# Eloquence

The craft of language that touches people deeply — and the discipline that separates it from purple prose.

**The core truth: eloquence is not the goal — resonance is. Form follows force.** Beauty is a byproduct of writing that is *precisely true*. When a sentence is exactly right, it sounds beautiful; reach for beauty first and it rings false. The trap is studying the masters' *outputs* (the anaphora, the chiasmus, the compressed metaphor) and mistaking them for *inputs*. They used those forms because the forms fit the truth they carried.

**The hierarchy of goals — never invert it:**

> 1. **Truth** — the thing is real and accurate
> 2. **Clarity** — understood without effort
> 3. **Force** — the sentence carries weight beyond its length
> 4. **Memorability** — it stays after the page is closed
> 5. **Beauty** — it is a pleasure in itself

Most bad writing chases #5 before earning #2. The masters reach #5 by perfecting #1–#4. Plain writing exposes weak thinking; ornament conceals it — so if you are not yet sure you've found the true thing, stay plain.

And the converse: clarity and resonance prove nothing. "Clarity and obviousness are not a sign of truth; they are the result of professionals at work" (Ilyakhov). Every device in this skill sells a pyramid as well as it sells the truth, which is why truth sits at #1.

## When to reach for this — and when not

**Reach for eloquence when:** the subject lives in the body, not the intellect — identity, death, love, justice, belonging, transcendence; the reader must *remember the line*, not just the idea; you need someone moved to act against their own comfort; or the form itself can make the argument (MLK's anaphora, Lincoln's antithesis).

**Check the context before the words:** the same line lands or becomes infamous depending on who says it, to whom, where and on what day ("Hang in there! Good mood and health to you!" from a prime minister to unpaid pensioners). And sometimes words are not the lever at all: a debtor owes creditors a phone call, not a better letter. → [references/clarity.md](references/clarity.md) §2.

**Stay plain when:** the reader is already motivated and just needs friction removed; the subject is practical (instructions, specs, a refund policy); or you're writing direct-response sales copy, where ornament *delays the decision* and signals insincerity. For that work, use the `marketing` skill — clarity and action win there.

## The operating procedure

Eloquence is a *finishing* craft. Never start here.

1. **Find the one true thing, in the reader's world** – one claim, true and not-yet-known to the reader, statable in a single sentence. Not "about loss" → "loss changes the size of ordinary rooms." If you can't state it, you haven't found it. The ornaments have nothing to attach to until this exists. Then check that it is about the reader, not the author: nobody cares what a company strives for; a mission or manifesto the reader cannot see themselves in is word-shuffling (→ [references/clarity.md](references/clarity.md) §3).
2. **Write it plain, as a picture** – subject, verb, object, no adjectives: *person + does + this way*. Then draft the whole piece in plain prose, each abstraction paired with an example the reader recognizes, and an anti-example where they would go wrong. The reader must see a film, not receive information. The iceberg must exist before you choose what to surface.
3. **Mark where force is missing** — read it aloud; flag every spot where energy drops, attention would wander, or a claim is *stated but not felt*. These — and only these — are the sites for technique.
4. **Choose the figure that fits the force** — each device is medicine for a specific failure: must be remembered → anaphora / chiasmus / monosyllabic gravity / a rhythmic slogan / a coined name; contrast must be felt → antithesis; abstraction must be seen → concrete image / governing metaphor / a plot the reader can feel from inside; must land harder → shorten it / omission; close must feel final → the kicker / chiasmus. → [references/craft.md](references/craft.md). Any metaphor that carries an argument the reader will act on gets the divergence audit: list what the source image implies that the subject does not share (→ [references/clarity.md](references/clarity.md) §5).
5. **Apply one figure. Read aloud. Decide.** — the test: does it sound *true*, or does it sound like it's *trying to sound true*? If you can hear the ornament as ornament, cut it. It's earned only when form and content fuse.
6. **Read the whole piece aloud, slowly** — the ear catches what the eye approves. Vary sentence length; replace sounds that fight their meaning; make the energy rise toward the end.
7. **Earn every ornament** — for each figure, remove it: does the sentence lose force, or gain clarity? If it gains clarity, the ornament was hiding something. Cut it.
8. **Test the closing line** — can it stand alone, no context, on a memorial stone or a poster, and still mean something? If not, write ten alternatives.

## The laws

- **Form follows force** — never apply a device to a vague idea; you only get vague technique.
- **Read it aloud** — not advice, quality control. Every master did; Churchill rehearsed like an actor.
- **Earn every ornament** — if you can hear the beauty *as* beauty, it hasn't fused with the truth yet.
- **Plain first, always** — find and state the true thing in plain words before any elevation.
- **Match register to stakes** — Churchillian rhythm on a trivial subject is pomposity. Write quietly when the subject is small.
- **One governing image per passage** — extend it; never import a second incompatible world (mixed metaphor).
- **Context decides** – speaker, relationship, place, day, tone. A perfect line in a broken context becomes a joke against you. Name the elephant before you speak ("You are probably thinking…").
- **The reader reads about themselves** – reflect what they already believe and want; change their mind inside, never in the headline. Side with them: whatever happened, the reader is not to blame.
- **A metaphor must not be the foundation of an argument** – the reader imports properties you did not mean (a broker is not a waiter). In a poem the metaphor is the experience; in persuasion it is a claim and gets audited.
- **Say it many times** – almost nobody heard it the first time. A line becomes immortal by repetition across years and angles, not by being said perfectly once.

## Composing with `marketing`

This is the optional **"make it sing"** pass — run it *after* the marketing skill has produced copy that is clear, true, and converting, and *only* on the parts where resonance matters (the headline, the manifesto, the brand line, the emotional peak — never the spec list, the guarantee, or the CTA mechanics). Eloquence elevates copy that already works; it cannot rescue copy that doesn't, and over-applied it lowers response. Earn the clarity first; then, where the subject deserves it, make it unforgettable.
