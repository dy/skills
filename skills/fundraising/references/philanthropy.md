# Philanthropy — raising from donors

This branch has something venture fundraising almost entirely lacks: real field experiments with random assignment, run on actual donors giving actual money. Where those exist they are cited below and they should outrank any practitioner claim, including the famous ones.

## The number that should organize everything

The AFP/Urban Institute Fundraising Effectiveness Project tracks donor data across thousands of US nonprofits. Two figures matter:

- Overall year-over-year donor retention sits around **43%**.
- First-time donor retention — the share who ever give a second gift — is around **18–19%**.

**Roughly four out of five first-time donors never give again.** Every acquisition campaign run by an organization that hasn't fixed stewardship is pouring water into a bucket with the bottom missing. If you are choosing where to spend effort, retention is almost always the higher-return work, and almost nobody does it because acquisition feels like progress.

## Donor-centered fundraising (Burk)

Burk's Cygnus research is the strongest survey work in the field: repeated studies over roughly two decades, tens of thousands of donors. Her finding is that donors have three concrete conditions for continuing to give:

1. **Prompt, genuinely personal acknowledgment** of the gift. Not a form letter.
2. **The gift attached to something specific** — a named program or project, narrower than the general mission.
3. **A report on what that specific gift accomplished, delivered before the next ask.**

This is survey research rather than a randomized experiment, but it converges with the FEP retention data, which is independent administrative evidence pointing the same direction. Treat the three conditions as the operating baseline.

## What the field experiments actually establish

| Finding | Study | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Seed money works, more than linearly** | List & Lucking-Reiley (2002), *Journal of Political Economy* — real university capital campaign | Announcing a higher share of the goal already committed raised both the participation rate and total dollars, with returns increasing faster than the seed share. **This is the empirical foundation of the quiet phase.** Secure and announce the lead before going public. A refund guarantee also increased giving. |
| **A match helps; a bigger match does not** | Karlan & List (2007), *American Economic Review* — over 50,000 prior donors by direct mail | Offering a match raised both response rate and revenue. But **$1:$1, $2:$1 and $3:$1 performed the same.** Announcing "triple your impact" buys nothing over "your gift is matched" and costs the matching donor three times as much. This directly contradicts standard practice. |
| **Overhead aversion is solvable by structure, not by austerity** | Gneezy, Gneezy & Keenan (2014), *Science* — 40,000 solicited donors | Donations fall as disclosed overhead rises **only when donors believe they personally fund the overhead**. Telling donors that a lead gift already covers administrative and fundraising costs roughly **doubled** the number who gave. Do not cut real capacity to protect a ratio; get a major donor to underwrite overhead and say so. |
| **Peer information raises gifts, up to a ceiling** | Shang & Croson (2009), *Economic Journal* — public radio pledge drives | Naming what a peer gave raised averages substantially (one condition moved roughly $121 to $172). But framing at the 99th percentile weakened it, because an unreachable number stops being a reference point. **Anchor aspirational but reachable.** |
| **Peer effects move the undecided only** | Frey & Meier (2004), *American Economic Review* | Social-proof messaging moved people who were previously indifferent. It did not move committed non-givers, and it did not move the already-committed. Target it accordingly instead of blasting it at everyone. |
| **Warm glow is why stewardship works** | Andreoni (1989, 1990) | Donors are "impurely altruistic": they value both the good produced and the private satisfaction of having given. This is why reporting impact back to a donor is not a courtesy — it is delivering the second half of what they came for. |

**One prominent principle that failed to replicate.** The identifiable victim effect — the basis for "show one named child, never statistics" — comes from Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (2007). A large-sample replication (Maier et al., 2023, *Collabra: Psychology*) **found no support for it**, nor for the related claim that pointing out the bias backfires. Use a single human story because it is concrete and true, which are independently good reasons. Do not present it as an evidence-backed law.

## Major gifts

**Panas's finding governs the whole discipline.** Interviewing donors who had made million-dollar-plus gifts, he found they uniformly already believed in the mission, respected the leadership, and were confident the gift would have measurable impact — *before* the solicitation. The ask converts belief; it does not manufacture it.

**Moves management** (Dunlop and Smith, Cornell, 1980s) is the field's uncontested operating model: **Identify → Qualify → Cultivate → Solicit → Steward**, then re-enter for an upgraded gift. Typical effort allocation is roughly a quarter on discovery, half on cultivation, well under a fifth on the actual solicitation, and the remainder on stewardship. A major gift can take up to two years. If your solicitation share is much higher than that, you are asking strangers.

The practical rules, from Panas and consistent across the field: ask **in person** when the sum justifies it, ask for a **specific amount** for a **specific purpose**, bring the right solicitor (usually a peer of the donor, often a board member rather than staff), and then **stop talking** after the ask. The silence is the donor deciding, and filling it is how solicitors talk themselves down.

## Capital campaigns

The structure is a direct analog of a venture round, which is worth noticing because it means the same physics apply.

- **Feasibility study first.** Interview 30–60 top prospects and board members before setting the goal, to test capacity against ambition. This is a go/no-go, not a formality.
- **Gift table.** Dollars concentrate steeply: the top handful of gifts typically carry half to two-thirds of the goal. The classic "80/20" framing is Pareto folklore applied to fundraising rather than a fundraising-specific law, and practitioners now widely report drift toward 90/10. The exact ratio is directional; the concentration is real, and the FEP pattern of dollars rising while donor counts fall corroborates it.
- **Quiet phase.** Solicit the top of the table before any public announcement. Well-run campaigns have **50–60% of the goal committed before going public** — which is precisely the List & Lucking-Reiley seed-money effect, operationalized.
- **Public phase.** Broaden to mid-level and peer-to-peer, typically over 12–24 months.

## Recurring and planned giving

**Monthly giving** changes donor identity, not just cash flow, and produces materially higher lifetime value than one-off annual gifts. The evidence here is practitioner synthesis rather than experiment, but it is consistent and the mechanism (habit and self-concept) is well grounded elsewhere.

**Bequests** are the most accessible planned gift — available at any income level, arranged in a will — and the most under-asked. The wealth-transfer figures used to justify planned-giving programs are broad macroeconomic estimates, so use them for direction and not as a forecast for your organization.

## Watch out

- **Acquiring donors you cannot steward is a net loss.** Fix retention before scaling acquisition.
- **Asking before belief exists** wastes both the gift and the relationship. Cultivation is not a delay; it is the work.
- **Don't buy a bigger match ratio.** The evidence says you're paying for nothing.
- **Don't starve overhead to protect a ratio.** Underwrite it and disclose that it's underwritten.
- **Don't present the identifiable-victim principle as settled.** It did not replicate.
- **Ethics are a professional standard here, not a vibe.** The AFP Code and the Donor Bill of Rights set the baseline: donors are entitled to know how gifts are used, to have their wishes honored, and to be free of pressure tactics. Overstating impact to close a gift is the philanthropic equivalent of securities fraud, and it ends careers in a field that is smaller and better connected than it looks.
