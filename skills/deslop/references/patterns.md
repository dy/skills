# Surface tells

Format: **Watch** (what fires) · **Fix** · **Exempt** (when it's not slop). Consolidate
stacked tells on one phrase into one finding. A pattern chosen deliberately in the
author's established voice is exempt everywhere.

## Vocabulary

### 1. Tier-1 words — near-zero tolerance
**Watch:** delve, tapestry, testament, paradigm shift, leverage (verb), synergy, myriad,
plethora, realm, beacon, multifaceted, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge,
harness (figurative), game-changer, cutting-edge, ever-evolving, seamless, groundbreaking.
**Fix:** the plain word (leverage→use, utilize→use, facilitate→help) or the concrete fact
the word is hiding.
**Exempt:** literal use (an actual tapestry, harnessing an actual horse); quoted text.

### 2. Tier-2 words — flag only in clusters (3+ in a piece)
**Watch:** robust, comprehensive, nuanced, innovative, crucial, pivotal, vital, vibrant,
intricate, showcase, underscore, highlight (verb), foster, garner, landscape (abstract),
interplay, enduring, enhance, valuable, key (adjective).
**Fix:** keep the one that earns its place; replace the rest with plain equivalents.
**Exempt:** a single occurrence doing real work.

### 3. Business jargon
**Watch:** navigate (complexities), unpack, lean into, deep dive, double down, circle
back, take a step back, on the same page, moving forward, at scale.
**Fix:** handle, explain, accept, analysis, commit, return to, reconsider, aligned, next.
**Exempt:** none — these never beat their plain equivalents.

### 4. Intensifier adverbs — cluster signal, not blanket ban
**Watch:** really, truly, deeply, fundamentally, genuinely, actually, literally, honestly,
incredibly, simply — plus *Importantly,* / *Crucially,* / *Interestingly,* as sentence
openers.
**Fix:** cut, or use a stronger verb/noun. An opener adverb announcing importance is
deleted; if the point matters, the sentence should show it.
**Exempt:** an intensifier carrying real contrast ("it literally caught fire").

### 5. Hyphenated-pair over-consistency
**Watch:** third-party, data-driven, high-quality, cross-functional, decision-making,
real-time, end-to-end hyphenated with perfect uniformity, including in predicate
position ("the report is high-quality").
**Fix:** drop predicate-position hyphens; let common pairs vary as humans do.
**Exempt:** technical compounds where the hyphen disambiguates.

## Grammar

### 6. Copula avoidance
**Watch:** serves as, stands as, functions as, acts as, represents, boasts, features,
offers — where *is/has* is meant.
**Fix:** is / has.
**Exempt:** a single instance in formal register; genuine role description ("she served
as chair 2019–2021").

### 7. Superficial -ing tails
**Watch:** trailing participle clauses adding fake depth: "…, highlighting the
importance of X", "…, reflecting the community's deep connection", "…, ensuring
alignment", "…, showcasing…".
**Fix:** delete the clause, or promote it to its own sentence with a named actor and a
sourced claim.
**Exempt:** participles describing actual simultaneous action ("she left, slamming the
door").

### 8. Passive voice and subjectless fragments
**Watch:** "No configuration file needed." "The decision was reached." "Mistakes were
made." "It is believed that…"
**Fix:** name the actor: "You don't need a configuration file." "The board decided."
**Exempt:** actor genuinely unknown or irrelevant; scientific-register conventions.

### 9. False ranges
**Watch:** "from X to Y" where X and Y sit on no meaningful scale ("from the Big Bang to
the cosmic web, from stars to dark matter").
**Fix:** a plain list of what's actually covered.
**Exempt:** real ranges with a midpoint ("from 3 to 8 weeks").

### 10. Synonym cycling (elegant variation)
**Watch:** the protagonist → the main character → the central figure → the hero, in
consecutive sentences.
**Fix:** repeat the word or use a pronoun. Repetition is human; rotation is a
repetition-penalty artifact.
**Exempt:** variation that adds information (a role name where the role matters).

### 11. Excessive hedging
**Watch:** stacked qualifiers ("could potentially possibly"), reflexive "it could be
argued", hedge density above ~8 per 1000 words in declarative writing.
**Fix:** one hedge per genuinely uncertain claim; commit everywhere else.
**Exempt:** calibrated academic hedging on genuinely contested claims.

## Content

### 12. Significance inflation
**Watch:** a pivotal moment, marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape,
indelible mark, setting the stage for, reflects broader trends, enduring legacy.
**Fix:** state what happened and let the reader judge significance; or cite who claims
it matters and why.
**Exempt:** sourced significance ("the ruling that created the four-factor test").

### 13. Promotional register
**Watch:** nestled, vibrant, breathtaking, stunning, rich (figurative), renowned,
must-visit, in the heart of, natural beauty, commitment to excellence.
**Fix:** neutral fact with a concrete detail ("a town of 12,000 with an 18th-century
church").
**Exempt:** actual marketing copy — but even there, specificity sells better than
adjectives; see the register guide.

### 14. Vague attribution / weasels
**Watch:** experts believe, industry reports, observers have noted, some critics argue,
many consider.
**Fix:** name the source ("a 2019 Chinese Academy of Sciences survey found…") or cut the
claim.
**Exempt:** none — an unnamed authority is not an authority.

### 15. Notability dumping
**Watch:** lists of outlets ("featured in NYT, BBC, Forbes"), follower counts, "active
social media presence" as proof of importance.
**Fix:** one concrete, dated instance with content ("in a 2024 Times interview she
argued…").

### 16. Formulaic sections
**Watch:** "Challenges and Future Prospects", "Despite these challenges… continues to
thrive", takeaway/bottom-line endings, sections with identical internal shape.
**Fix:** concrete events with dates and causes; end when the content ends.

### 17. Generic positive conclusions
**Watch:** the future looks bright, exciting times ahead, journey toward excellence, a
major step in the right direction.
**Fix:** a concrete plan or fact, or nothing.

### 18. Invented specificity
**Watch:** numbers, names, studies, and dates that appear in the rewrite but not the
source — added to "be more specific".
**Fix:** `[ADD: which study? what number?]` placeholders. This is the one pattern where
the fix is to *add* a visible gap.
**Exempt:** never.

### 19. Knowledge-cutoff leakage
**Watch:** "as of [date]", "while specific details are limited", "based on available
information", "up to my last update".
**Fix:** state what is known plainly; cut the disclaimer.
**Exempt:** a real journalistic dateline ("as of press time, the count stood at…").

## Format and style

### 20. Decorative separators and dash overuse
**Watch:** `·` as a separator in navigation, metadata, or inline lists; `—`, `–`, ` -- `
as prose punctuation. Budget: 0 middle dots and 0 dashes in short copy, ≤2 dashes in a
long piece. The dash-as-dramatic-aside ("the catalog, honestly priced,") is a tell even once.
**House rule (hard):** never emit a middle-dot separator (`·`) or em-dash (`—`); both are
primary AI tells. When a dash is genuinely warranted, use an en-dash (`–`) instead. This
holds even inside the ≤2 dash budget.
**Fix:** normal punctuation, a new line, or restructure. **Not** parentheses (the swap
preserves the aside habit).
**Exempt:** working documents — reference files, specs, agent/skill instructions, routing
tables, navigation furniture — where the character is structural rather than rhetorical
(SKILL.md → Scope); mathematical use of `·`; a user writing sample that uses them; ranges
(pp. 3–9); established house style.

### 21. Bold overuse
**Watch:** mid-sentence bold on phrases, bold as emphasis rhythm, bold on every list
lead.
**Fix:** unbold; if the point needs emphasis, restructure so position provides it.
**Exempt:** docs highlighting a term at first definition; UI strings.

### 22. Inline-header bullet lists
**Watch:** `- **Label:** sentence restating the label`, three to five in a row, each the
same shape.
**Fix:** prose, or a real list where items are parallel data, not restatements.
**Exempt:** reference docs and API docs — term-definition structure is legitimate
there; strip the puffery inside the definitions instead.

### 23. Emoji decoration
**Watch:** 🚀 in headings, ✅ on bullets, emoji as section markers.
**Fix:** delete.
**Exempt:** platforms where emoji are the register (some chat/social contexts) and the
author's sample uses them.

### 24. Title Case Headings
**Watch:** All Main Words Capitalized in headings.
**Fix:** sentence case.
**Exempt:** the publication's house style (AP/Chicago titles).

### 25. Curly quotes in the wrong context
**Watch:** “smart” quotes in code, config, plain-text files, or mixed with straight
quotes in one document.
**Fix:** straight quotes; consistency.
**Exempt:** typeset prose — curly quotes are typographically correct there.

### 26. Dramatic headings
**Watch:** "The X Trap", "The Silent Killer", "Why X Destroys Everything", "The Hidden
Cost of X" — could it be a thriller chapter title?
**Fix:** a heading that states the content ("Retry storms under load").

### 27. Fragmented headers
**Watch:** a heading followed by a one-line paragraph restating the heading before the
real content starts.
**Fix:** delete the warm-up line.

### 28. Over-bulleting
**Watch:** connected reasoning chopped into bullets; every section a list.
**Fix:** prose for reasoning; bullets only for genuinely parallel items.

## Communication artifacts

### 29. Chatbot leakage
**Watch:** "I hope this helps!", "Certainly!", "Of course!", "Would you like me to…",
"let me know if…", "Here is a…" — correspondence pasted as content.
**Fix:** delete. Zero tolerance.

### 30. Sycophancy
**Watch:** "Great question!", "You're absolutely right", "That's an excellent point".
**Fix:** respond to the substance directly.

### 31. Signposting
**Watch:** "Let's dive in", "Here's what you need to know", "Now let's look at",
"without further ado", "In this post we'll cover…"
**Fix:** do the thing instead of announcing it.
**Exempt:** a genuine table of contents in long-form docs.

### 32. Filler phrases
**Watch → Fix:** in order to → to · due to the fact that → because · at this point in
time → now · has the ability to → can · it is important to note that → (cut) · it's
worth noting → (cut) · when it comes to X → (restructure) · at the end of the day →
(cut) · in today's fast-paced world → (cut).

### 33. Recap endings and engagement bait
**Watch:** conclusions that summarize what was just said; "What do you think?";
"Let me know in the comments."
**Fix:** end on the last real point.

### 34. Reassurance kickers
**Watch:** "And that's okay.", "There's nothing wrong with that.", unsolicited comfort.
**Fix:** delete — the reader didn't ask for permission.

### 35. Sentence-opener tics
**Watch:** "So," / "Look," / "Honestly?" / "Here's the thing:" as fake-candid hooks;
habitual And/But openers; paragraph after paragraph opening with a transition word
(transition-opener density >30% is a statistical tell).
**Fix:** start with the content.
**Exempt:** an occasional And/But opener for real contrast — the tic is the frequency.

### 36. Rhetorical question answered immediately
**Watch:** "What makes an API good? It comes down to predictability."
**Fix:** the statement alone ("Good APIs are predictable"), or let a real question
stand unanswered.

### 37. Forced metaphor, then explained
**Watch:** an invented metaphor followed by its own decoding ("the codebase is a garden
we must tend — in other words, delete unused code").
**Fix:** keep whichever half carries the meaning (usually the plain half); delete the
other.

## Clarity (after Ilyakhov, «Ясно, понятно»)

Model prose fails the reader by being abstract, not only by being ornamental. These fire
where the reader is supposed to *understand* something; a summary or conclusion after the
picture has been drawn is exempt.

### 38. Abstraction without a picture
**Watch:** a paragraph of claims with no acting person, named object, number or scene;
an abstract or inanimate subject plus a state verb ("consistency is a key driver of
growth", "the platform serves as a foundation for collaboration"); chains of verbal nouns
(-tion, -ment, -ance). Ilyakhov's test: could the reader *see* it? "The degree of a
consultant's attentiveness is the determining factor of sales growth" shows nothing.
**Fix:** person + does + this way, using an actor and an act the source contains ("the
more carefully a consultant answers, the more often customers come back"). If the source
gives no example, leave `[ADD: example?]`. Never invent one.
**Exempt:** specialist readers who already hold the picture; a generalization that follows
an example; deliberate summary.

### 39. Class nouns standing in for things
**Watch:** solutions, offerings, opportunities, measures, initiatives, resources, various,
a range of, a variety of.
**Fix:** name the things from the source ("a Kremlin tour, dinner, a boat ride", not
"various events"). Missing → `[ADD: which?]`.
**Exempt:** a genuine category in a definition or a legal text.

### 40. Author's-world framing
**Watch:** openings and purposes centered on the author: "we're excited to announce",
"we've rebuilt our website", "our journey", "this post aims to highlight / showcase /
inform"; year-end copy that counts the author's activity (lines of code, cups of coffee).
"A text cannot have the task of informing" (Ilyakhov).
**Fix:** lead with what changes for the reader, using the source's facts; move the
author's news to the end or cut it.
**Exempt:** genuine personal writing where the author is the subject the reader came for.

### 41. Unanchored and over-precise numbers
**Watch:** a large number with no comparison or use-scenario nearby ("we launched 20 new
courses", "5.8 trillion"); decimals and odd percentages in lay prose ("34.2% of users").
**Fix:** a comparison or scenario the source supports; round to a spoken mark ("every
third user", "half an hour"). Never invent the comparator.
**Exempt:** accounting, specs, research, specialist readers who check figures.

### 42. Talking down to the reader
**Watch:** negative second person ("you failed to…", "if you lack the willpower…"),
labels for people (lazy, clueless, amateurs), in-group openers ("good writers know…",
"most people get this wrong", "there's a type of founder who…"), scolding vocatives.
**Fix:** side with the reader; explain the error by circumstance, not stupidity. All
else equal, give good outcomes to "you" and bad ones to "they".
**Exempt:** a voice built on harshness that the author chose and the audience came for
(an opinion column); direct feedback someone asked for.
