# Offers — build and price what you sell

The order of operations: make the offer worth far more than the price (Value Equation), then de-risk it (guarantee), then enhance it (scarcity, urgency, bonuses), then name it. A lead magnet is "the offer-for-the-offer" — the free thing that gets a stranger to raise their hand before you sell anything. A great offer beats great copy; no amount of writing rescues a weak bundle at a wrong price.

---

### The Value Equation — Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers*

*Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice) — raise the numerator, crush the denominator to charge premium prices.*

**When:** Diagnosing why an offer under-converts or under-prices, or designing a new offer and deciding where to invest improvement effort.

| Component | Meaning | How to apply | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dream Outcome *(raise it)* | The specific feeling and life-change the prospect already imagines — not what the product does, what they envision their life becoming. | Name the outcome in the prospect's own language. Use before/after contrast. Paint the identity shift: "You'll be the person who…" Put the dream outcome in the offer title itself. | "$99/month gym membership" vs. "Lose 20 lbs in 6 weeks or pay nothing" — same delivery, vastly different perceived value. |
| Perceived Likelihood of Achievement *(raise it)* | The customer's belief that *they specifically* will get the result — not whether the product works in general. Often the highest-leverage lever because delivery quality changes slowly, but perception shifts immediately. | Stack social proof specific to the exact avatar (same age, same situation, same objection). Use before/after case studies with precise numbers. Build a proprietary process name. Layer a strong guarantee so perceived risk of failure collapses. Address the "yes, but I'm different" objection directly. | "Sarah, 47-year-old mom of 3 with no prior gym experience, lost 22 lbs in 8 weeks using this program." The specificity makes the reader think: *that's exactly me.* |
| Time Delay *(lower it)* | The gap between payment and first meaningful result. Shorter gaps = higher perceived value. | Deliver a quick win within the first 24–48 hours. Add a fast-start bonus, onboarding call, or immediate access component. Name the quick win explicitly in the offer. | A coaching program with a "Day 1 Kickoff Call" where the client walks away with a personalized plan immediately — value experienced before the program officially starts. |
| Effort & Sacrifice *(lower it)* | The perceived work, lifestyle changes, and things the customer must give up. Done-for-you is priced higher than do-it-yourself because effort is a cost. | List every step the customer must take. Remove or automate as many as possible. Explicitly name what they do NOT have to do: "No meal prep, no counting macros, no gym intimidation." | A meal delivery service prices at 4–6× per meal vs. groceries because it eliminates planning, shopping, prepping, and cooking — the product's value is the effort removal. |

**Templates:**

```
Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)
```

```
Offer audit: score each lever 1–10. Which denominator is highest? That is the priority fix.
```

> `[Avatar] achieve [specific measurable result] in [time frame] without [biggest feared sacrifice]`

> `Within [24 hours / first session / Day 1], you will have [concrete deliverable] so you can [immediate benefit]`

> `[Name], [age], [situation identical to prospect], achieved [exact result] in [exact timeframe] using [method name]`

**Watch out:**
- Confusing features with dream outcomes — describing what the product IS rather than what the customer will BECOME
- Treating all four levers equally — in most markets, perceived likelihood is the most neglected and highest-leverage variable
- Claiming massive dream outcomes while offering no proof of likelihood — creates disbelief, not desire
- Adding friction back through complicated onboarding while promising effortlessness
- Inventing time-delay claims ("results in 24 hours") that cannot be delivered — destroys trust on fulfillment

### The 5-Step Grand Slam Offer Creation Process — Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers*

*Start from the dream outcome, map every problem between the customer and it, invert each problem into a solution, design delivery vehicles for each solution using the Delivery Cube, then trim high-cost/low-value items and stack the remainder into a bundle.*

**When:** Creating a new offer from scratch, or re-packaging an existing service that is not converting or not commanding premium prices.

- **Step 1: Identify the Dream Outcome** — Pick the single most desirable result your market wants. This is your north star and your offer's headline. Spend time in customer language: read reviews, listen to sales calls, note the exact phrases people use. Do not use internal product language. *Apply:* The outcome must be specific, emotionally resonant, and measurable. *e.g.* Hormozi's gym clients did not want "a gym membership" — they wanted to lose weight. Shifting the stated dream outcome from "gym access" to "lose 20 lbs" changed the offer's entire architecture and pricing ceiling.

- **Step 2: List Every Problem** — Map every obstacle the customer faces before buying, while engaging with your product, and after it ends. *Apply:* Write problems in three time phases: (1) before — logistical and psychological barriers to beginning; (2) during — obstacles while doing the work; (3) after — what stops sustaining results or causes regret. Aim for 20+ items. Each unsolved problem is a reason they say no or ask for a refund. *e.g.* For a weight-loss program: before = "I don't know what to eat," "I've failed before so I don't believe I'll succeed." During = "I lose motivation by week 3." After = "I regain the weight once the program ends."

- **Step 3: Turn Problems into Solutions** — Take each problem from Step 2 and write its solution-oriented inverse. The solution is not a product feature — it is the customer's problem rephrased as a solved state. *Apply:* For each problem, write: "How do I [verb] so that [customer] can [solved state] without [specific obstacle]?" *e.g.* "I don't know what to eat" inverts to "A done-for-you weekly meal plan so they never have to decide what to eat." "I lose motivation by week 3" inverts to "A weekly accountability check-in call at week 3 specifically."

- **Step 4: Choose Delivery Vehicles (The Delivery Cube)** — For each solution, brainstorm delivery using six dimensions: (1) client-service ratio (1:1, small group, 1:many); (2) client involvement (DIY, done-with-you, done-for-you); (3) support channel (text, email, phone, Zoom); (4) medium (live, recorded, written, audio, video); (5) speed/response time (async vs. real-time); (6) pricing structure (10× price / 1/10 price mental model to unlock new formats). *Apply:* Run each solution through each dimension. Ask: what combination produces the highest perceived value at the lowest fulfillment cost? Done-for-you raises perceived value but raises cost; recorded content has near-zero marginal cost once created. *e.g.* "Weekly accountability call" could be 1:1 calls (high perceived value, high cost), group coaching calls (moderate perceived value, low marginal cost), or async check-in via text (low cost, lower perceived value).

- **Step 5: Trim and Stack** — Plot all solutions on a 2×2 of value-to-customer vs. cost-to-deliver. Remove high-cost/low-value first. Remove low-cost/low-value next. Keep high-value items regardless of cost. Bundle everything kept into a named, stacked offer with individual dollar values assigned to each component. *Apply:* For each remaining item, assign a credible standalone price. Sum the component prices into a "total value" number. Set the offer price well below the total to create a price-to-value gap. Present the stack with line-item values before revealing the bundled price. *e.g.* Hormozi's gym weight-loss bundle: 6-week workout program ($500) + done-for-you meal plan ($300) + weekly group accountability calls ($1,200) + supplement kit ($200) + 24/7 Slack access ($600) + bonus recipe book ($150) + 1:1 kickoff call ($401) = **$4,351 total value, priced at $599**.

**Templates:**

```
Step 1 headline: '[Avatar] achieve [dream outcome] in [timeframe] without [biggest feared sacrifice]'
```

```
Step 2 prompt: 'List everything that could stop [avatar] from achieving [dream outcome] before they start,
while doing the work, and after the program ends.'
```

```
Step 3 inversion: 'Problem: [obstacle]. Solution component: [how we eliminate that obstacle specifically].'
```

```
Step 4 Delivery Cube matrix: For each solution, write:
[1:1 / group / recorded] × [DIY / DWY / DFY] × [live / async] = [cost estimate] / [perceived value estimate]
```

```
Step 5 value stack:
'[Component name] ($[value]) + [Component name] ($[value]) + ... = Total value $[X]. Your investment: $[Y] — a savings of $[X minus Y].'
```

**Watch out:**
- Starting with the product you already have instead of the dream outcome — leads to rationalizing existing components rather than designing around actual customer problems
- Listing too few problems in Step 2 — 5–8 problems produces a thin offer; aim for 20+ to find non-obvious value opportunities
- Assigning inflated, indefensible dollar values to components in Step 5 — Hormozi explicitly warns against pricing a one-hour PDF at $5,000; it destroys credibility
- Skipping the trim step and keeping everything — bloated fulfillment cost kills margins and overwhelms customers
- Failing to name individual components — unnamed components feel like filler; named components feel like distinct, high-value assets

### The Guarantee Taxonomy — Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers*

*Four guarantee types — unconditional, conditional, anti-guarantee, and implied/performance — that can be stacked to reverse customer risk and increase offer conversion.*

**When:** An offer is not converting because perceived risk is too high, or when designing the risk-reversal layer of any paid offer.

- **Unconditional Guarantee** — A no-questions-asked refund window. The customer pays, tries the product, and can get their money back for any reason within the specified period. Strongest risk-reversal for cold audiences because there is zero condition the customer must satisfy to claim it. *Apply:* Set a time window (30, 60, or 90 days). Make the claim process dead simple — one email, no forms, no explanation required. Name it memorably so the guarantee feels like an asset, not a footnote. *e.g.* Hormozi's illustrative "Club a Baby Seal Guarantee": "After 30 days, if you wouldn't club a baby seal to stay a customer, you don't pay a penny." The absurdity makes the guarantee memorable and signals absolute confidence in the product.

- **Conditional Guarantee** — A guarantee tied to specific actions the customer must take. Protects against non-serious buyers while still reversing risk for committed customers. *Apply:* The formula: "If you do [specific actions] and do not achieve [specific result] within [timeframe], we will [specific remedy — refund / work free until result / pay X]." Define the condition precisely and make it achievable by any serious customer. *e.g.* "If you complete all six modules, attend all live calls, and implement the weekly assignments, and you don't close at least one new client within 90 days, we will work with you one-on-one at no additional charge until you do — or refund you in full."

- **Anti-Guarantee** — An explicit "all sales are final" policy with a compelling reason why. Used when the product reveals proprietary methods, business processes, or information that cannot be returned once consumed. *Apply:* State clearly that all sales are final. Immediately provide a "reason why" the customer can understand and agree with — typically involving your exposure. Only use this where the reason is genuinely credible. *e.g.* "Because we'll be showing you the exact internal systems and supplier relationships that drive our business — proprietary information we've spent years and millions developing — all sales are final. Once seen, this cannot be unseen, which means we can't offer refunds."

- **Implied / Performance Guarantee** — Any arrangement where the provider only gets paid when the customer gets a result. Revenue share, profit share, trigger-based bonuses, and ratchets are all forms. The guarantee is baked into the deal structure, not stated explicitly. *Apply:* Structure compensation as: base (small or zero) + performance component tied to a specific outcome. Define the metric precisely (revenue, leads, ranked keywords, etc.). Use for high-ticket B2B and agency relationships where you control the inputs. *e.g.* "We charge zero upfront. When our ads generate a sale, we take 15% of that transaction. If we generate nothing, you pay nothing." Hormozi used this model in his gym marketing business to land clients who would never pay a flat retainer.

**Templates:**

```
Unconditional: 'Try [product] for [X] days. If you're not [specific positive feeling], email us and we'll
refund every penny — no questions, no forms, no hassle.'
```

```
Conditional: 'If you [specific actions customer must take] and do not [specific result] within [timeframe],
we will [specific remedy — refund / work free until result / pay X].'
```

```
Anti-guarantee: 'All sales are final. Here's why: [specific reason involving your exposure or the
irreversibility of the value]. Once you [experience / learn / receive this], it cannot be returned —
which is also why it's worth [price].'
```

```
Performance/Implied: 'We get paid [X%] of [metric] we generate. Zero upfront. Zero if we deliver zero.
Our incentive is exactly the same as yours.'
```

```
Guarantee stack: '[Unconditional 30-day money-back] + [Conditional: if you do X and don't hit Y within
90 days, we work free until you do]' — layer both for maximum risk reversal.
```

**Watch out:**
- Using an anti-guarantee to cover a weak product — customers will detect the mismatch between the bold policy and the thin offer
- Burying the guarantee in fine print — a guarantee only raises conversion if the customer actually reads and believes it; feature it prominently
- Conditional guarantees with conditions so onerous that no real customer can qualify — this reads as a fake guarantee and destroys trust
- Performance guarantees without a clean measurement mechanism — disputes arise when both parties disagree on whether the condition was met
- Ignoring that guarantees can be stacked — a single weak unconditional guarantee when a stack (unconditional 30-day + conditional 90-day) would dramatically increase perceived safety

### Offer Enhancers: Scarcity, Urgency, Bonuses, and the MAGIC Naming Formula — Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers*

*Four enhancement layers applied on top of the core offer to increase perceived value and accelerate the buying decision. Apply these after the core offer, problem-solution stack, delivery, and guarantee are already built.*

**When:** Final layer of offer design — after the core offer is solid. These are accelerants, not foundations.

> ⚠️ The 50–60% of sales in the final four hours figure is a practitioner heuristic reported across multiple summaries, not a cited study — treat as directional.

- **Scarcity** — Limiting the number of units, clients, or spots available. Real scarcity — not manufactured — is the most ethical and effective form. *Apply:* Three mechanisms: (1) Total business cap — "We only take 10 new clients at a time because each gets direct access to [founder / lead coach]." (2) Cohort cap — "This intake is limited to 25 participants to maintain group quality." (3) Growth-rate cap — "We bring on a maximum of 3 new clients per week." The most honest tactic: simply tell people how close you are to your real cap — this creates social proof alongside scarcity. *e.g.* "This cohort closes Friday at midnight or when the 30 slots fill — whichever comes first. As of this morning, 22 of 30 are taken." Honest (if true), creates urgency, and functions as social proof simultaneously.

- **Urgency** — Limiting the time window in which an offer is available or at a given price. Scarcity limits quantity; urgency limits time. *Apply:* Four types: (1) Rolling cohort — program starts on a fixed date and the next one is months away. (2) Seasonal urgency — the offer aligns with a natural calendar event. (3) Promotional urgency — a limited-time price or bonus package with a hard deadline. (4) One-time offer — presented only at the point of sale, never available again at that configuration. Always use real deadlines; fake countdown timers destroy long-term credibility. *e.g.* Hormozi's "$100M Money Models" launch: "Offer closes Monday, 11:59 PM PT." Fixed, real, and non-negotiable — no extensions, which trained the audience that his deadlines are real.

- **Bonuses** — Additional items added to the core offer to increase the perceived value gap without discounting the main price. Core principle: **never discount — add bonuses instead.** A single offer broken into named components with individual prices is worth more than the same offer presented as one price. *Apply:* For each bonus: (1) Name it specifically — the name should contain the benefit. (2) Explain how it relates to the prospect's specific problem. (3) Assign a credible, defensible standalone price. (4) Announce bonuses *after* the prospect has already said yes to the core offer — "one more thing" sequence. (5) Add scarcity and urgency to bonuses themselves: "This bonus is only available to the first 10 buyers." Stack bonuses so the last one is the one that tips the decision. *e.g.* "When you join today, you also get: The 7-Day Meal Prep Blueprint ($197 value) — so you never spend more than 30 minutes planning food for the week. The Plateau-Breaker Protocol ($97 value) — the exact 3-day reset I use when clients stall. 30-Day Private Slack Access ($300 value) — message me directly when you hit any obstacle. Total bonus value: $594. Yours free when you enroll today."

- **MAGIC Naming Formula** — A five-component acronym for naming offers: **M** = Magnet (a magnetic reason why the offer exists — a number, method name, or bold claim), **A** = Avatar (who it is specifically for), **G** = Goal (the dream outcome), **I** = Interval (the timeframe to achieve the result), **C** = Container word (a word that bundles it into a recognizable format). Use 3–5 of the 5 components — not all five are always needed. Container words: Blueprint, Masterclass, Challenge, Cohort, Accelerator, Bootcamp, Intensive, System, Formula, Method, Program, Sprint. *e.g.* "The 6-Week Fat-Loss Accelerator for Busy Moms Over 40" — M: "6-Week" (implies a proven, time-bound system), A: "Busy Moms Over 40" (exact avatar), G: "Fat-Loss" (dream outcome), I: "6-Week" (interval), C: "Accelerator." Compare to "Weight Loss Program" — which communicates none of those things.

**Templates:**

```
Scarcity: 'We only accept [N] new [clients / members / participants] [per month / per cohort].
As of today, [X] of [N] spots are taken.'
```

```
Urgency: 'This offer closes [specific date and time, e.g., Friday June 20 at 11:59 PM ET].
No extensions. No exceptions.'
```

```
Bonus stack reveal:
'Enroll today and you also receive:
[Bonus 1 Name] ($X value) — [one sentence on what it is and what problem it solves].
[Bonus 2 Name] ($X value) — [...].
Total bonus value: $X. Available only to [first N buyers / those who enroll by date].'
```

```
Bonus pricing rule: Assign the price the bonus sells for on its own, or the market rate for equivalent
work. Never invent a price you cannot defend.
```

```
MAGIC formula: '[Number or bold claim] + [Specific Avatar] + [Dream Outcome Verb-Noun] + [Timeframe] + [Container Word]'
Example fill-in: '[21-Day] [Real Estate Investor] [Deal-Closing] [Sprint]'
```

**Watch out:**
- Using fake scarcity or fake countdown timers that reset — audiences recognize these patterns and they actively damage trust
- Discounting the core offer instead of adding bonuses — discounting trains buyers to wait for sales and signals the original price was inflated
- Assigning indefensible bonus prices — claiming a 1-hour PDF is worth $5,000 destroys the credibility of the entire value stack
- Announcing bonuses before the core offer is sold — bonuses close the gap between "interested" and "buying"; announcing them too early dilutes their closing power
- MAGIC naming failure: using all 5 components when 3 would be sharper — "The Proven 6-Week High-Performance Busy Professional Corporate Fat-Loss Transformation Accelerator System" is unspeakable; pick the 3 most differentiating elements
- Naming without the avatar — generic container words like "Blueprint" or "Masterclass" without a specific who feel like commodity offers

### The Value Ladder — Russell Brunson

*An ascending bait → frontend → middle → backend offer architecture that lets you acquire customers at break-even on a low-ticket offer and generate margin as they ascend to high-ticket products.*

**When:** Designing an entire product ecosystem or funnel architecture — before writing any individual offer. A single product with no upsell path means ad spend can never break even, making scaling impossible.

- **Bait** — A free or near-free offer designed entirely for acquisition, not revenue. Its job: get a stranger to exchange contact information or a tiny payment, creating a customer relationship that did not exist before. *Apply:* Design something with high perceived value to the prospect but low delivery cost to you — a free PDF, a free book (pay shipping), a $7 "tripwire" product. The bait must solve one real problem completely, creating a positive first experience that makes the next rung feel like the obvious next step. *e.g.* Brunson's *DotCom Secrets* book: free, prospect pays $7.95 shipping. Brunson loses money on each unit — he pays for the book and fulfillment. The value is the email address and the relationship. The book ends with natural transitions to ClickFunnels software.

- **Frontend** — The first real paid product, typically $27–$297. This is where acquisition cost is recovered. The customer has self-selected as a buyer — a fundamentally different psychological status than a lead. *Apply:* Price to cover ad spend and convert the most enthusiastic bait takers. Deliver genuine value — enough that the customer feels they underpaid. Add an order bump (a complementary low-friction add-on at checkout) to increase average order value without adding friction. *e.g.* ClickFunnels software at $97/month is the frontend. After the free book, interested readers land in a funnel where the software solves the exact problem the book described. The book was an extended demo.

- **Middle** — Upsell offers between frontend and backend that deepen the customer relationship and deliver more of the same transformation. Revenue here is where profit typically begins. *Apply:* Identify the natural "what's next?" question a frontend buyer asks. Offer the answer as a complementary course, membership, or done-with-you service. Present immediately after purchase (one-click upsell) while buyer energy is highest. *e.g.* Funnel Hacking Live event ticket ($997) sits mid-ladder between ClickFunnels software and coaching programs. It solves "I have the tool but I need community and live instruction."

- **Backend / Peak** — High-ticket offers ($5,000–$100,000+) for the most committed customers who want proximity, accountability, or done-for-you service. A small percentage of customers generate the majority of revenue here. *Apply:* Create mastermind groups, one-on-one coaching, or done-for-you agency services. Sell only to customers who have ascended previous rungs — they have proof you deliver, which eliminates the hardest part of selling high-ticket. The primary sales tool at this level is a phone call or application, not a page. *e.g.* Brunson's Two Comma Club Coaching ($25,000) and Inner Circle Mastermind ($50,000/year). Only customers who have succeeded with ClickFunnels are offered these. The ascent filters for fit and commitment.

**Templates:**

```
Value Ladder mapping:
'Our bait is [free/near-free offer]. It solves [one specific problem] and leads naturally to [frontend product],
which solves [next problem]. Customers who want [deeper outcome] ascend to [backend], and those who want
[done-for-you/proximity] can access [peak].'
```

**Watch out:**
- Building a single product instead of a ladder — no upsell path means ad spend can never break even, making scaling impossible
- Treating the backend as an afterthought — most businesses never design it, leaving 60–80% of lifetime customer value on the table
- Pricing the bait so high it stops functioning as an acquisition mechanism
- Failing to build a natural content bridge from one rung to the next — each rung should answer the "what's next?" question the previous rung creates

### Lead Magnet Creation (The Offer-for-the-Offer) — Alex Hormozi, *$100M Leads*

*Create a free, complete solution to a narrow problem that is so valuable people would pay for it — use it to convert strangers into engaged leads who have already experienced your value before you sell anything.*

**When:** Before running any paid ads or cold outreach at scale. Your lead magnet is the asset every channel drives traffic to — build it first, then build the channels. Also use when conversion rates on any channel are low; usually the problem is a weak or absent lead magnet. Cross-reference the Core Four acquisition channels in [distribution.md](distribution.md).

- **Pick the Narrowest Possible Problem** — Your lead magnet solves one specific, urgent, named problem — not a category. The narrower the problem, the more relevant it feels and the higher the opt-in rate. *Apply:* Write down your core offer's biggest result. Work backwards: what is the single most painful sub-problem that stands between your prospect and that result right now? *e.g.* Core offer: full SEO retainer for e-commerce brands. Lead magnet problem: "Your product pages are cannibalising each other in Google search." Lead magnet: "Keyword Cannibalisation Audit Checklist for Shopify Stores."

- **Choose a Lead Magnet Type** — Three types: (1) Reveal-the-problem — diagnose an issue the prospect did not know they had. (2) Sample or trial — give a single-serving taste of the core offer. (3) One step of a multi-step process — deliver one high-value step; the rest requires purchasing. *Apply:* Reveal-the-problem works best when the problem gets worse the longer they wait (free site speed audit, posture screening). Sample/trial works best for consumables or SaaS (free trial, first module free). One-step works best for multi-phase programs (give module 1 of a 6-module course). *e.g.* A web agency uses a free "Page Speed Report" (reveal-the-problem) that shows exactly how much revenue the prospect is losing per second of load time — and the pain grows daily.

- **Solve It Completely — No Fluff** — The lead magnet must deliver a real, actionable result. Watered-down content destroys trust. *Apply:* After drafting, ask: "Could I charge $50 for this and have people feel it was worth it?" If no, add more specificity, more examples, or more steps until the answer is yes. *e.g.* Instead of "Tips for Better Email Subject Lines," deliver "47 subject line formulas with open-rate benchmarks and one fill-in-the-blank template for each — tested across 2M sends."

- **Name It for the Outcome, Not the Format** — The name is the hook. It must state the specific result or reveal the specific problem — not describe what format it is. *Apply:* A/B test at least two names before scaling traffic. Name formula: [Specific outcome or problem] + [Timeframe or qualifier if it strengthens specificity]. *e.g.* "The Cold Email Audit: 11 Reasons Your Outreach Gets Ignored (and the fix for each)" outperforms "Cold Email Guide."

- **Include a Strong CTA Back to the Core Offer** — The lead magnet must bridge explicitly to the paid offer. Consume-and-done is wasted momentum. *Apply:* Final page or section: "You now know [what you just gave them]. If you want [core offer result] without [remaining friction], [specific CTA — book a call / start free trial / apply now]." *e.g.* At the end of the keyword-cannibalisation checklist: "You've found the conflicts. Fixing them across a 500-page store takes ~40 hours. We do this in 5 days — here's how: [link to service page]."

**Templates:**

```
Lead magnet name:
'The [Target Audience] [Specific Problem] [Format]: [# of] [specific tactics/fixes] to [achieve outcome] in [timeframe]'
```

```
Lead magnet CTA (final page):
'You now know [what the lead magnet revealed]. If you want [end result of core offer] without [main remaining obstacle],
[action verb] here: [link].'
```

```
Reveal-the-problem type:
Free [audit/test/assessment] that shows [prospect] exactly how much [money/time/growth] they are losing to
[specific problem] — and what to do next.
```

```
Sample type:
'[First module / free trial / sample] of [core offer] — get [specific result] in the next [short timeframe],
then decide if you want the full [offer name].'
```

**Watch out:**
- Making the lead magnet too broad ("The Ultimate Marketing Guide") — no one opts in because it promises everything and delivers nothing specific
- Delivering watered-down content to "protect the paid offer" — this destroys trust; give away secrets, sell the implementation
- No CTA inside the lead magnet — the lead consumes it and disappears with no bridge to the core offer
- Driving traffic to the core offer directly instead of the lead magnet — strangers need a low-friction first step before they are ready to buy
- Never testing the name — the name is 80% of the opt-in rate for the lead magnet landing page
