# Content — what the page says, and how to find it

Copy ([copy.md](copy.md)) is the phrasing engine. Positioning ([positioning.md](positioning.md)) decides what is true. This file sits between them and answers the question neither one does: **what should this page actually say, in what order, and where do the ideas come from?**

**Governing heuristic — the ideal ad is no ad.** Altshuller's Ideal Final Result says the ideal machine performs its function without existing. Applied here: before asking "what claim do we make," ask "what would make the claim unnecessary?" A product whose value is self-evident in five minutes needs a page that gets people to minute five, not a page that argues. Every block below is a confession that something is not yet self-evident. Add each one deliberately.

**Prime rule:** you are not inventing content, you are *harvesting* it. Every block on a good page is an answer to a question a real customer already answered. Skip the harvest and you will write adjectives.

---

## 0. The procedure

Run in order. Steps 1–3 are research; do not generate angles before finishing them.

1. **Harvest the truth** — voice-of-customer mining plus a contemplate run (§1). Produces raw material in the customer's own words.
2. **Ladder every claim** — climb from attribute to the value it serves (§2). Produces the emotional promise that belongs at the top of the page.
3. **Diagnose** — pin the reader's Awareness stage and market Sophistication ([copy.md](copy.md) §1). This decides *which blocks are mandatory and in what order* (§5).
4. **Generate angles** — run the engine (§4), not a stare-at-the-wall session. Discard the first batch.
5. **Select blocks** — choose from the repertoire (§3) by job, never by "pages usually have this."
6. **Sequence** — order by awareness stage (§5).
7. **Audit** — evidence grades (§6) and failure modes (§7). Cut every block that does not answer a real objection.

## 1. Harvest the truth

### The one diagnostic that feeds the whole page

A single interview or survey, if the questions hit each layer, produces nearly every block. Ask a *recent* buyer, never a hypothetical one, and ask for stories rather than opinions: story questions surface concrete quotable language, opinion questions surface adjectives indistinguishable from your competitor's.

| Ask this | Feeds this block |
|---|---|
| "What was going on in your life that brought you here?" (Wiebe's signature question) | Hero — the situation, verbatim |
| "Tell me about the day you started actively looking. What happened right before?" (Moesta switch interview) | The trigger moment, the problem section |
| "Describe a specific moment when this was worst." (Price, forces specificity over platitude) | Agitation bullets |
| "Why does that keep happening? What's underneath it?" | Root cause / "why this happens" |
| "What has this already cost you this year? Who else gets affected?" (SPIN Implication) | The price of inaction |
| "Why is that important to you?" — repeated 3–5 times (laddering) | The terminal-value promise |
| "What almost stopped you?" | Objection ledger, guarantee |
| "What were you worried you'd lose by giving up your old way?" | Switching-is-easy block |
| "What would you have used instead if we didn't exist?" | "Instead of X" block, and your real competitive set |
| "If you could cut that from three days to three hours, what would that free you to do?" (SPIN Need-payoff) | Outcome promise |

Mine existing sources too: reviews, support tickets, forum threads, sales-call recordings. The language already exists; you are transcribing, not composing.

### Contemplate → page brief

A [contemplate](../../contemplate/SKILL.md) run already produces most of this. Its questions map onto page blocks almost one-to-one, so a contemplation on the repo *is* a content brief. This also satisfies contemplate's own rule that insight without an act gets refused.

| Contemplate question | Block it feeds |
|---|---|
| "Show me one person's exact frustration — the precise moment it hits" | Hero situation + agitation |
| "What's the villain in the user's story? What would they complain about to a friend?" | Problem section, in their words |
| "Who is NOT the user? Who must be disappointed?" | The disqualifier |
| "What does it replace?" | "Instead of X, just Y" |
| "Why now — what changed, what's closing?" | Urgency, without a fake timer |
| "What friction does this remove? What becomes possible that wasn't before?" | Outcome promise |
| "What would the world lose if this never existed?" | Terminal-value promise |
| Essence in one sentence (PROJECT artifact) | Hero headline |
| The boundary — what it must NOT become | Honest limits |
| Kill list / verdict (REVISE artifact) | What to cut from the page |
| The dodged question (BLINDSPOTS artifact) | Usually the objection you have not answered |

## 2. Ladder every claim

**Means-end chain / laddering — Gutman (1982), *Journal of Marketing*; technique formalized by Reynolds & Gutman (1988).** The only framework in this file with genuine peer-reviewed origin and decades of independent replication.

People buy **attributes** for their **consequences**, and consequences for the **values** they serve. Climb the ladder by asking "why does that matter to you?" until answers stop producing new content.

```
Attribute (what it is)  →  Functional consequence (what it does)
   →  Psychosocial consequence (how it makes me feel / how I'm seen)
      →  Terminal value (security, competence, belonging, autonomy)
```

Worked: quilted extra-ply → you never worry about running out → you are a relaxed, in-control host → competence and security. That ladder is why the line is *"we sell cleanliness, not toilet paper."* Levitt's "people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole" is the same move stopped one rung early.

**Where each rung goes on the page** — this maps onto vertical hierarchy almost perfectly:

| Rung | Placement |
|---|---|
| Terminal value | Hero headline / brand promise |
| Psychosocial consequence | Which testimonials you choose, and how you frame them |
| Functional consequence | Mid-page benefit bullets, the "so that…" under each feature |
| Attribute | Spec list, furthest down |

Competitors match functional benefits easily. Identity-level values are far harder to copy, which is why the top rung converts and the bottom rung commodifies.

**Do not confuse laddering with the 5 Whys.** They repeat a question but climb opposite directions for opposite purposes. The 5 Whys (Toyoda/Ohno, Toyota) drills *backward into causation* and terminates at a fixable root cause; use it on the product side to write "why this actually happens," which is your differentiation. Laddering climbs *forward into meaning* and terminates at a value; use it on the customer side to write the promise.

## 3. The block repertoire

Choose by the job the reader needs done, never by convention. Each block is a debt: it costs attention, so it must retire a real objection.

| Block | The job it does | Include when | Mechanism | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Situation trigger** (hero) | Opens on the *moment* the need fires, not the product category | Almost always | JTBD: people hire a product to make progress in a circumstance | Naming a demographic instead of a moment ("for marketing teams") |
| **The reframe** | Tells the reader the problem is not what they think it is | Sophisticated markets, category creation | Challenger "commercial insight" | A reframe with no evidence behind it is just a contrarian pose |
| **Terminal-value promise** | The one emotional sentence at the top | Almost always | Top rung of the ladder (§2) | Skipping to values without earning them reads as vapor |
| **Outcome promise (WIIFM)** | States the concrete result and by when: "in 3 months you can pass a Google interview" | Whenever the outcome is provable | SPIN Need-payoff | Promising an outcome you cannot evidence is the fastest trust loss on the page |
| **What you tolerate without it** | Names the daily indignities the reader has normalized | Problem-aware readers who have stopped noticing the pain | Moesta's *push* of the situation | Listing annoyances the reader does not actually feel |
| **The price of inaction** | Compounds the cost forward: what this costs by Q4, who else it hits | High-consideration purchases; right before the CTA | SPIN Implication questions, pre-answered | Invented numbers. Use the customer's own figures or none |
| **Root cause ("why this happens")** | Explains the mechanism behind the problem | When competitors treat symptoms | 5 Whys | Pseudo-science mechanism invented for marketing purposes |
| **Wrong / instead / right now** | What you are doing wrong, what to do instead, what to do today | Educational and content-led pages | Teaching creates reciprocity and authority | Condescension. Requires you be genuinely right |
| **The DIY objection** | "Why pay if I can do this myself?" answered honestly: yes you can, here is the time, skill and failure risk it costs | Any product with a plausible manual alternative | Opportunity-cost framing | Pretending they *can't* when they obviously can. Concede first, then price the difference |
| **The disqualifier** | "Who this is NOT for; when you don't need it" | Almost always. Underused | Two-sided messaging; qualification; contrast | A disqualifier that excludes nobody real ("not for people who hate saving money") is theater and reads as smug |
| **Instead of X, just Y** | Positions against the incumbent behavior, not only rival products | When the real competitor is a spreadsheet, a habit, or nothing | JTBD's real competitive set | Naming a rival you cannot beat on the stated axis |
| **Life with it** | Shows people or businesses visibly better off | Aspirational and consumer categories | Upward social comparison | Tipping into smugness produces malicious envy and resentment, not desire |
| **Why haven't you done it yet?** | Names the excuse (no time, tried before, too complex) and answers it. The answer *is* the product | Mid-page, after desire exists | Objection surfacing | Naming an excuse the reader does not hold makes you look like you are guessing |
| **The 2-of-3** | Give the evidence and let the reader draw the conclusion; do not state it | Skeptical, expert audiences | Self-generated arguments persuade harder than received ones | Being so oblique the reader draws the wrong conclusion. Requires the inference be genuinely obvious |
| **How it works** | The mechanism, concretely | Whenever the claim strains belief | Tangibility; Hopkins' reason-why | A diagram that explains nothing |
| **Proof** | Retires doubt with evidence matched to the specific doubt | Always | Proof hierarchy: customer case > demo > data > vision ([persuasion.md](persuasion.md)) | Generic logos. Proof from the wrong cohort signals misfit |
| **Objection ledger / FAQ** | Answers the real anxieties in the buyer's words | Always | Moesta's *anxiety of the new* | FAQ as marketing filler ("Why is your product so great?") |
| **Switching is easy** | Migration, import, "keep your existing X" | When an incumbent tool or habit exists | Moesta's *habit of the present* | Underclaiming effort, which produces churn and refunds |
| **Honest limits** | What it does not do, who it fits badly | Technical and expert audiences | Credibility through admitted weakness | Fake modesty about a trivial flaw fools nobody |
| **Pricing rationale** | Why it costs what it costs | Premium pricing, or price far from category norm | Reason-why | A rationale that reveals the price is arbitrary |
| **CTA** | One action, named plainly, friction stated | Always | — | Multiple competing CTAs; "Learn more" |

## 4. The angle engine

Do not stare at a blank page. Generate systematically, then converge.

**Two rules that come from actual research, and change the architecture:**

- **Generate alone, pool afterward.** Interacting groups produce fewer and worse ideas than the same people working separately and combining results (Diehl & Stroebe 1987; Mullen et al. 1991 meta-analysis). The cause is *production blocking*: while one person talks, the others lose their thread. Practically: run several independent generation passes, then merge, then converge. Never one continuous freeform session.
- **Discard your first ten angles.** The serial-order effect is among the most replicated findings in the divergent-thinking literature (Beaty & Silvia 2012 and replications): later ideas are reliably more original, because people exhaust the obvious category first and only then switch search strategy. Treat the opening batch as warm-up and take the *second* batch as your candidates.

### The grid (Zwicky morphological analysis)

The workhorse. Define parameters, list values, sample cells deliberately across the space rather than clustering near the obvious corner.

| Parameter | Values |
|---|---|
| Audience | each real segment you have evidence for |
| Pain | each diagnosed pain, from the harvest |
| Moment | the trigger circumstances (JTBD) |
| Emotion | relief · pride/status · fear of falling behind · trust · curiosity |
| Format | question · number · story · comparison · demo |
| Proof | case · demo · data · guarantee · founder story |
| Objection | the specific one this angle retires |

Two layers compose: use the idea methods below to generate distinct **insights**, then run each insight through this grid to generate distinct **executions**.

### Varying one insight across four axes

Given a single true insight, cross it against: **(a) awareness stage** (Schwartz — unaware needs the problem named, most-aware needs only the offer), **(b) emotion targeted**, **(c) format**, **(d) objection answered**. One insight yields dozens of executions without a new truth claim.

### Generators, in descending order of evidential support

- **Morphological grid** (above) — systematic coverage, no creativity required.
- **Far-domain analogy** (Synectics; Gordon) — "make the familiar strange." Evidence is genuinely mixed: distant analogies raise the ceiling on novelty but also the variance, so use as one generator among several and validate by convergence rather than trusting the leap.
- **SCAMPER applied to the angle, not the product** — substitute the claimed benefit or the messenger; combine two pains; adapt a proven angle from an adjacent category; magnify one attribute into the whole hook; put a feature built for one purpose to work on another pain; eliminate a category convention ("no sales call, ever"); reverse the usual promise ("helps you manage *less*").
- **Reversal / worst-possible-angle** — write the worst conceivable ad, then invert it. Negation is easier to generate fluently, and the exaggeration exposes the real objection hiding underneath.
- **TRIZ contradiction** — treat marketing tensions as contradictions to resolve rather than compromises to split ("premium price vs. mass-market trust" resolves via a free tier that builds trust before the price appears).
- **James Webb Young's five steps** — gather raw material, work it over, *drop it entirely*, let the idea arrive, then shape it against constraints. The incubation step is not mysticism; it matches the serial-order finding.
- **De Bono's lateral thinking** (provocation, random entry, Six Thinking Hats) — usable as a cheap prompt generator, but be honest: it has essentially no controlled empirical support and has been characterized by cognitive psychologists as untested. Do not cite it as method.

### Converging

Separate divergence from convergence in time; never blend them. Score each surviving angle on: **is it a cliché in this category** · **does it name a concrete person and moment** · **is there real proof behind it** · **does it match the reader's awareness stage**. Kill everything that fails one.

## 5. Sequencing by awareness stage

The reader's stage decides the order, not taste. (Full model in [copy.md](copy.md) §1.)

| Stage | Open with | Blocks that earn their place | Omit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | The situation and its cost | Situation trigger · what you tolerate · price of inaction · root cause | Price, feature lists, comparisons |
| **Problem-aware** | The pain, in their words | Agitation · root cause · reframe · outcome promise · mechanism | Category education |
| **Solution-aware** | The mechanism that makes yours different | Instead of X · comparison · proof · disqualifier · DIY objection | Problem education, which insults them |
| **Product-aware** | The product name plus one superiority claim | Proof · objection ledger · switching is easy · pricing rationale · guarantee | Pain agitation |
| **Most-aware** | The offer | CTA · guarantee · scarcity if genuine | Everything else |

## 6. Evidence grades for common tactics

This file grades its own advice. Most of what circulates as landing-page wisdom is correlational content-analysis or vendor marketing. Four tactics below have real controlled or meta-analytic backing; the rest are conditional, and two should not be asserted at all.

**Strong enough to state as established:**

| Tactic | Evidence | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Specificity / precise numbers** | Janiszewski & Uy (2008, *Marketing Science*): precise anchors produce smaller adjustment and read as more credible under uncertainty | Ceiling exists. Loschelder et al. (2016, *Psych Science*) found over-precision reads as cold and can hurt rapport. Hopkins on specificity is practitioner lore, not study |
| **The disqualifier / two-sided messaging** | Crowley & Hoyer (1994, *JCR*): two-sided messages beat one-sided on credibility, especially with skeptical audiences already aware of the negative. Ein-Gar, Shiv & Tormala (2012, *JCR*), the actual "blemishing effect" study | Dose-dependent, and **order matters**: the negative must come *after* the positives, and the effect holds under low-effort processing. Under high-stakes deliberation, or if the negative leads, it reverses |
| **Benign-envy social comparison** | van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Pieters (2011, *PSPB* and *JCR*): benign envy drives purchase; malicious envy drives hostility toward the brand | The featured customer must read as **similar** and their success as **earned and attainable**. Otherwise you manufacture resentment |
| **Comparative "instead of X"** | Grewal et al. (1997, *Journal of Marketing*) meta-analysis: more attention, processing and purchase intent than non-comparative. Direction is solidly corroborated; the specific effect sizes circulating are not verifiable against the primary text | Real costs: lower source believability, less favorable attitude toward the ad, and it boosts recall of the *competitor* you named. This is a **challenger tactic** — leaders avoid naming challengers for exactly that reason. Claims must be substantiated, or you take on legal risk alongside the credibility loss. Note the research base is 1990s print and broadcast; SaaS "vs" pages are an untested extrapolation |

**Conditional — do not state as universal:**

| Tactic | Verdict | What the evidence actually shows |
|---|---|---|
| Questions in headlines | MIXED, with real causal evidence for direction | Lai & Farbrot (2013, *Social Influence*) is a genuine randomized field experiment on live classified ads: question headlines beat declarative, and **self-referencing questions using "you/your" beat plain questions**. Direction is credible; the circulated magnitudes conflict wildly across secondary write-ups, so cite the direction only. Petty, Cacioppo & Heesacker (1981) explains the boundary: questions raise *scrutiny*, so they help a strong argument at low involvement and **backfire** on a weak one, or when they interrupt a highly-engaged reader already forming favorable thoughts |
| Digits in titles | WEAK | Plausible mechanism (a number signals scope and scannability) and some correlational support, but no clean causal test, and the flagship "36% more clicks / Conductor" stat is untraceable to any primary report |
| Countdown timers | MIXED, **HARMFUL when fake** | Genuine scarcity is real (Worchel, Lee & Adewole 1975), and the one clean documented win (CXL/Speero, +27% revenue) used a **real** operational cutoff. No credible isolated test shows a fake timer works regardless of truth, and GoodUI documents an aggressive-deadline test that *lost* 4% of purchases. The EU's 2022–23 CPC sweep found dark patterns on 148 of 399 screened shops, 42 of them running fake or resetting timers specifically. Named in the FTC's 2022 dark-patterns report; blacklisted under the EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive |
| Price of inaction | WEAK | Loss aversion is under serious live challenge: Gal, Rucker & Shavitt (2018, *JCP*) re-examined its pillars and found status-quo bias, the endowment effect and low-stakes affect ratings do not support a universal law. The published rebuttal concedes the core point, that it is contingent on moderators. SPIN's Implication finding is real but single-house, unreplicated, observational, and drawn from live B2B *conversations*, never tested on written copy. Use the framing, drop the certainty |
| Testimonial format claims | MIXED | Social proof itself is among the best-established findings, and Spiegel/Northwestern (2017) shows purchase likelihood rising sharply with review count, **more at high price points than low** — so proof matters most where risk is highest. But the confident format rules ("video beats text," "named-with-photo beats generic," "peer beats generic") have **no isolated controlled test** behind them. GoodUI's video-testimonial pattern rests on five underpowered tests; the video claim traces to vendors selling video |
| Long vs short copy | WEAK / contested | No adequately powered test isolates length; every famous case on both sides changed several variables at once. The often-cited 37signals result was a page-length *and* testimonial-photo change, reported at 47% in the primary write-up and inflated to "102.5%" as it circulated. Benchmark data comparing short and long pages conflates length with offer complexity. The honest position: length is a symptom of how much argument the reader's awareness stage requires, not a lever. The price/complexity/traffic-temperature moderation is grounded in the Elaboration Likelihood Model, not in landing-page data |
| Guarantees | MIXED | The mechanism is legitimately grounded in services-marketing research (Hart 1988; Wirtz & Kum 2004 on how rarely guarantees are actually abused), and GoodUI has five real tests at large samples. But the pooled result is underpowered and the effect size is not public. Every circulating headline number ("21% lift," "doubled conversion," "12% refund rate") is untraceable folklore. Expect the benefit to scale with perceived risk |
| Clarity beats persuasion / 5-second test | MIXED | NN/g's F-pattern and scanning research is legitimate, but it shows people *scan*, not that clarity outranks persuasion. No study pits a clear-unpersuasive page against a persuasive-confusing one. Krug is expert heuristic, explicitly not experiment |

**Do not assert:**

- **The 2-of-3 self-persuasion move.** The generation effect (Slamecka & Graf 1978) and Aronson's self-persuasion work are real, but they concern memory encoding and attitude consistency. No experiment tests the specific copy move of stating two points and letting the reader infer the third. It is a plausible extrapolation on real adjacent psychology. Use it, test it, never cite it as proven.
- **"Video lifts conversion 80%."** Traced to EyeView Digital's self-published white paper, no peer review, no disclosed methodology, vendor with a direct interest in selling video. A zombie stat. Video plausibly helps complex high-consideration purchases and plausibly hurts simple fast ones; the number is fiction.

**Better-evidenced tactics missing from most landing-page advice** — reach for these before the fashionable ones:

- **Anchoring** (Tversky & Kahneman 1974) — among the most replicated findings in decision research. Directly applicable to price framing. **But not its decoy/asymmetric-dominance elaboration**: Yang & Lynn attempted 91 replications across 23 product categories and only 11 succeeded. Anchor, yes; build a pricing page around a decoy tier, no.
- **Serial position** (Murdock 1962; Glanzer & Cunitz 1966) — the U-shaped recall curve is one of the most reliable results in cognitive psychology. Strongest point first, the ask last, and never bury either in the middle. This is the same mechanism as the "beginning and end" stimulus in [persuasion.md](persuasion.md), arriving from a different literature.
- **Descriptive-norm social proof** (Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius 2008; Schultz et al. 2007) — large field experiments with measured behavior, far stronger than testimonial research. State what similar others actually do.
- **Friction and form-field reduction** — HubSpot's analysis across roughly 40,000 forms plus the documented Expedia single-field case; convergent and consistent. Usually beats any copy tweak.
- **Mere exposure** (Zajonc 1968; Bornstein 1989 meta-analysis of 208 experiments, r ≈ 0.26) — repetition reliably increases liking. Modest effect, relevant to retargeting and repeat visits.
- **Foot-in-the-door micro-commitment** (Freedman & Fraser 1966; Burger 1999 meta-analysis) — small first commitments raise later compliance. Applies to multi-step forms and quizzes.
- **First-impression speed** (Lindgaard et al. 2006, *Behaviour & Information Technology*) — visual appeal judgments form in about 50 milliseconds and hold steady at longer exposures. This one is genuinely peer-reviewed and heavily cited, unlike the design statistic usually quoted beside it.

**Zombie statistics — never repeat these:**

- *"Attention span is now 8 seconds, shorter than a goldfish."* Fabricated. Traces to an SEO site posing as a research institute; the BBC found it baseless.
- *"Video increases conversion 80%."* Vendor white paper, above.
- *"Numbers in headlines get 73% more engagement" / "36% more clicks."* No locatable primary report behind either, despite the confident "Conductor study" attribution.
- *"Odd numbers outperform even numbers."* Traces to a single uncited line on Outbrain's own help page justifying its own listicle titles, then copied outward until it read as a finding. BuzzSumo's 100-million-headline dataset, the only large real one, found **10** performed best. The folklore is contradicted by the best available evidence.
- *"The Rule of 7."* No traceable origin study exists; the number was fixed by a consultant. Where effective-frequency research was actually done it converged nearer **3**, and modern data on interactions before conversion ranges from about 4 to 17 depending on category. The repetition principle is real via mere exposure; the "7" is invented.
- *"Users read 20% of a page."* Real NN/g finding but bounded ("at most 28%, 20% more likely"), from a study of 25 highly-literate users, routinely mis-cited as a law.
- *"94% of first impressions are design-related."* No source reports this. It appears to be a corrupted, roughly doubled echo of Fogg et al. (2002, n=2,684), which found design was cited in **46.1%** of *credibility* comments — a different measure entirely. Usually attributed to "a Stanford study" and parked next to the genuine Lindgaard 50ms finding to borrow its credibility.
- *"Reduce choices — the paradox of choice."* The jam study does not replicate; Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010) meta-analysis found an average effect near zero. Do not build a page around it.

## 7. Failure modes

- **Stacking every block.** A page carrying all twenty-one signals desperation. Each block must retire a named objection; if you cannot name it, cut the block.
- **Harvesting nothing.** Adjectives are what you write when you skipped §1. If no sentence on the page came out of a customer's mouth, start over.
- **A disqualifier that disqualifies nobody.** It only buys credibility if a real, desirable-looking segment is genuinely turned away.
- **Invented numbers in the price-of-inaction block.** Use the customer's own figures or drop the block entirely. This is the single easiest place to lose a technical audience.
- **Laddering to a value the product does not deliver.** The top rung must be earned by the bottom rungs; otherwise the page promises identity and delivers a feature.
- **Generating before diagnosing.** Angles produced before the awareness stage is pinned will be aimed at the wrong reader, and no amount of craft fixes that.
- **Treating consultant frameworks as science.** SPIN and Challenger rest on large proprietary datasets never released for independent reanalysis; Gap Selling, Sandler, MEDDIC and JTBD are seasoned pattern-recognition, not controlled research. Say "Rackham's research found," never "research proves." Only laddering here carries peer-reviewed backing.
