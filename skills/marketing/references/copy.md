# Copy — words that sell

Copy is downstream of diagnosis. Always pin the prospect's Awareness stage and the market's Sophistication level first (Section 1) — they determine which headline type will land (Section 2) and how hard you'll need to work to make claims believable (Section 3). Pour the result into the right structural skeleton (Section 4), then layer in the pitch mechanics (Section 5). Skip diagnosis and you're writing for yourself, not the reader. For offer construction see [offers.md](offers.md); for positioning the product before writing see [positioning.md](positioning.md); for Cialdini principles that layer into any structure see [persuasion.md](persuasion.md).

## 1. Diagnose (before you write a word)

### Five Stages of Market Awareness — Eugene Schwartz
*Diagnose how much your prospect already knows about their problem, the solution category, and your product — then open your copy at exactly that point in their internal conversation.*

**When:** Before writing any headline, ad, landing page, or email. Identify which stage your target prospect occupies, then select the matching lead strategy. A Stage 1 approach on a Stage 5 market sounds naive; Stage 5 on a Stage 1 market is incomprehensible.

| Stage | Who they are | The copy move | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Unaware** | Does not recognize they have a problem; has accepted it as normal | Never open with the product, category, or problem. Open with a story, striking fact, or curiosity-gap pattern interrupt that pulls them into a new way of seeing their situation. Schwartz: "You are selling nothing, promising nothing, satisfying nothing. Instead, you are echoing an emotion." Avoid: price, product name, logo, any direct statement of what the product does. | "There's a strange reason why — no matter how hard you try — your body fights every diet you've ever attempted." |
| **2 — Problem Aware** | Feels the pain but has not identified a solution category | Name and validate the problem with precise empathetic language — make the prospect feel seen ("Finally, someone understands this"). Introduce the possibility a solution exists without hard-selling. Stay in the prospect's own vocabulary of suffering. | Headline: "Why Can't I Sleep — No Matter What I Try?" |
| **3 — Solution Aware** | Knows a solution type exists; doesn't yet know your product | Crystallize the desire and solution so sharply the prospect instantly recognizes "this is what I've been looking for." All three elements — desired outcome, solution type, your product's fit — must be implied within the headline or first line. Use testimonials and case histories to show your product is the best representative of the category. | "At Last — A Simple Way to Fall Asleep in Under 10 Minutes, Without Pills or Gimmicks." |
| **4 — Product Aware** | Knows your product exists but isn't yet convinced | Display the product name prominently in the headline or logo. Use the headline to: sharpen product image, overcome a specific objection, prove superiority, reinforce desire, extend image to new contexts, introduce new proof, or announce a new mechanism. Schwartz: "Display the name of the product — and use the remainder of the headline to point out its superiority." | Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce ad: "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock." |
| **5 — Most Aware** | Knows your product, already inclined to buy — needs a reason to act now | State little more than the product name and a compelling offer (price, deal, deadline, bonus). The headline IS the offer. No elaborate education needed. | "Kindle Paperwhite — Now $99. Today Only." |

**Templates:**

```
Stage 1 (Unaware):
[Striking fact or story hook that reframes their situation]... and here's why that matters to you.

Stage 2 (Problem Aware):
Why do [symptom the prospect lives with] no matter how hard you [effort they're already making]?

Stage 3 (Solution Aware):
At last — a [adjective that differentiates] way to [desired outcome] without [chief objection].

Stage 4 (Product Aware):
[Product Name]: [single superiority claim that overcomes the top objection or sharpens the image].

Stage 5 (Most Aware):
[Product Name] — [offer: price / deadline / bonus]. [One-line urgency reason].
```

**Watch out:**
- Writing a Stage 5 (offer-only) headline for a Stage 1 prospect — generates zero engagement
- Writing a Story/Hook lead for a Stage 5 prospect who just wants the price — wastes their time and looks evasive
- Mis-diagnosing the stage by thinking about what YOU want to say rather than what the prospect already knows
- Lumping all Problem-Aware prospects together — different sub-segments may be at Stage 2 or Stage 3 depending on prior research
- Confusing Awareness (how much they know) with Sophistication (how jaded they are) — two separate diagnostic axes

### Five Levels of Market Sophistication — Eugene Schwartz
*As more competitors pile into a market making the same claim, the copy move that works escalates from a plain claim, to an amplified claim, to a mechanism, to a better mechanism, to identification with the prospect's identity — diagnose where your market is and make the right move.*

**When:** Use when choosing WHAT TYPE of headline argument will land. Awareness tells you where the prospect's mind is; Sophistication tells you how worn-out the market is from similar advertising. A crowded, mature market demands a more advanced move. A genuinely new category can use the simplest move.

| Level | Situation | The copy move | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — State the Claim** | Your product or category is genuinely new; no competitor has made this claim before | State the core benefit claim directly in the headline. No mechanism, proof architecture, or amplification needed — the novelty carries it. | "This New Pill Gives You All-Day Energy." |
| **2 — Enlarge/Amplify the Claim** | Competitors have entered with similar claims | Take the winning Level 1 claim and push it to its absolute limit. Add specificity — numbers, timeframes, magnitudes. Outbid competitors on the same dimension. Do NOT introduce a new mechanism yet. Schwartz: "Copy that first successful claim — but enlarge on it. Drive it to the absolute limit. Outbid your competition." | "Lose 47 Pounds" or "Twice the energy in half the time." |
| **3 — Introduce a Mechanism** | Market saturated with big claims; prospects sceptical | Name the mechanism in the headline — the HOW. A Level 2 headline says WHAT; Level 3 adds HOW. The mechanism makes the claim credible by giving it a causal explanation. | "I AM 61 POUNDS LIGHTER… NEVER A HUNGRY MINUTE" (mechanism: appetite suppression, not starvation). Modern: "Lose 30 Lbs With the 2-Minute Morning Cortisol Reset Protocol." |
| **4 — Elaborate or Upgrade the Mechanism** | A competitor has introduced their own mechanism | Take the successful mechanism concept and make it easier, faster, more certain, solving more of the problem, overcoming a known limitation, or delivering additional benefits. Schwartz: "Simply elaborate or enlarge upon the successful mechanism. Make it easier, quicker, surer; allow it to solve more of the problem; overcome old limitations; promise extra benefits." | "The Only Probiotic That Survives Stomach Acid to Reach Your Intestine — 10x More Live Cultures Delivered Where They Work." |
| **5 — Identification with the Prospect** | Market completely saturated; no claim or mechanism is believed | Abandon promise-driven copy. Open by naming the prospect's dominant thought, feeling, frustration, or identity. Do NOT lead with desire or mechanism — lead with identification. Schwartz: "The emphasis shifts from the promise and the mechanism which accomplishes it, to identification with the prospect himself. You are dealing here with the problem of bringing your prospect into your ad — not through desire — but through identification." | "For People Who Are Serious About Their Smile." / "The Deodorant for Men Who Sweat Hard and Don't Apologise for It." |

**Templates:**

```
Level 1: [Benefit claim]. [Product Name] finally makes it possible.

Level 2: [Specific number or magnitude of benefit] — [timeframe or condition that makes it more impressive].

Level 3: [Specific result] — with [named mechanism that explains HOW].

Level 4: The [adjective: only / new / faster / smarter] [mechanism] that [overcomes the known limitation of existing mechanisms / delivers extra benefit].

Level 5: For [identity/tribe label]. / [Dominant emotion or thought they're already having, stated back to them].
```

**Watch out:**
- Using a Level 1 (plain claim) move in a Level 3+ market — the claim lands as unbelievable
- Jumping to Level 5 identification in a fresh market where a simple claim converts better — over-engineering
- Confusing Sophistication with Awareness — they are separate axes; a market can be Problem-Aware (Stage 2) but already Sophisticated (Level 3)
- Introducing a mechanism at Level 3 but making it generic ("our proprietary formula") rather than named and specific
- At Level 5, still sneaking in a benefit claim or mechanism — the prospect detects the pitch and disengages; identification must be pure

### Mass Desire Principle — Eugene Schwartz
*Copy cannot create desire — it can only channel the mass desire that already exists in millions of hearts and focus it onto your product.*

**When:** Use before writing a single word of copy: identify which pre-existing desire (fear, hope, dream, frustration) your product taps into, select the single strongest one, and make that desire — not your product — the hero of your headline and lead.

**Components:**

- **Identify the Mass Desire** — Mass desire is "the public spread of a private want" — an emotion felt by millions simultaneously. It pre-exists your product. List every desire your product could satisfy. Rank by (a) intensity — how urgently does the prospect feel this? (b) mass — how many people feel it? (c) timing — is this desire growing or fading right now? *Example: A financial newsletter appeals to desire for security, desire for wealth, fear of loss, desire for insider knowledge. In a bear-market year, fear of loss is most intense — lead with that.*

- **Select the Single Dominant Desire for the Headline** — You can only serve one desire in the headline. Schwartz: "You need to single out the strongest desire that your product fulfills and work with it in your ad." Pick the one that is (a) most intensely felt by the largest segment right now and (b) most directly satisfied by your product's actual mechanism. The product is the instrument; the desire is the story. *Example: "How to Protect Everything You've Saved — Before the Next Crash." The product (newsletter) is not mentioned. The mass desire (protect savings from catastrophe) is the entire headline.*

- **Channel, Don't Create** — Schwartz: "The power, the force, the overwhelming urge to own that makes advertising work, comes from the market itself, and not from the copy." Never argue a prospect into wanting something they don't already want. Find the desire that is already active and show precisely how your product is the vehicle for fulfilling it. The copy's job is the focusing lens, not the light source. *Example: A gym equipment ad doesn't create the desire for a fit body — it finds the prospect who already desperately wants that.*

**Templates:**

```
Mass Desire headline formula:
[Emotion/state the prospect already craves or fears] — [your product] is how [they get it / it stops].

Desire identification prompt:
"What does my prospect lie awake at night wanting to happen — or terrified will happen? That is my headline."
```

**Watch out:**
- Featuring the product instead of the desire — "Introducing the XR-7 Resistance Band System" instead of "The Body You Want in 20 Minutes a Day at Home"
- Picking a desire that is real but not intense enough — "convenience" rarely motivates like "freedom" or "security"
- Trying to serve multiple desires in one headline — splits the prospect's attention
- Selecting a desire the product cannot actually satisfy — raises desire the product then disappoints

## 2. Headlines (where 80¢ of the dollar is spent)

### Caples Headline Formula Categories — John Caples
*Rank and write headlines by their primary appeal type — self-interest first, news second, curiosity only when combined with one of the first two.*

**When:** When writing any direct-response headline, ad, email subject line, or landing page title — especially when you need to choose which angle to lead with.

| Category | Meaning | How to apply | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Self-Interest** *(test first)* | Promises a specific benefit or desired outcome; directly answers "what's in it for me" | State the exact outcome the reader wants. Use benefit nouns (money, health, time, status) paired with a result verb. The promise must be specific enough to be believable. | "How to Double Your Energy Before 9am — Without Coffee or Drugs" |
| **News** | Announces something genuinely new. Trigger words: Introducing, Announcing, New, Now, At Last, Finally, Just Released. | Lead with an announcement word, state what is new and why it matters. Combine with a benefit for maximum pull. | "Announcing a New Kind of Retirement Account the IRS Can't Touch" |
| **How-To** | Signals a practical payoff — reader will learn something actionable | Start with "How to" + the exact thing the reader wants. Optionally add a qualifier that removes the main objection. | "How to Win Friends and Influence People" |
| **Question** | Opens a knowledge gap; forces self-identification ("is this me?") | Ask a question your specific reader is already asking themselves. Avoid questions they can answer "no" to and walk away. Use "Do you", "Are you", "Which of these", "Why do". | "Are You Ever Tongue-Tied at a Party?" |
| **Command** | Opens with an imperative verb; creates urgency and clarity | Lead with an action verb. Follow with the direct benefit. Keep it short — commands lose force with padding. | "Throw Away Your Oars!" |
| **Testimonial** | Uses real customer voice to carry the benefit claim | Use actual customer language in quotation marks. Include a specific result or number. Optionally attribute to a named person with a descriptor. | "I was ashamed of my English until my friend gave me this book" |
| **Reason Why** | Leads with a numbered list of reasons; the word "why" signals explanation and proof | State a number of reasons + the claim, or open with "Why [topic]". The number creates implicit specificity. | "7 Reasons You Should Never Buy Term Life Insurance" |
| **Curiosity** *(only combined with self-interest or news)* | Creates an open information gap. Caples warned this is the weakest category alone — arouses curiosity but doesn't sell unless it connects to a benefit or news element. | State an incomplete or paradoxical claim that implies a significant payoff. Always ensure the resolution delivers on a self-interest angle, not just surprise. | "The Secret the Health Food Industry Doesn't Want You to Know" |
| **Price / Offer** | Features a specific price, discount, free offer, or easy-payment plan. Works in price-sensitive markets and for closing-stage copy. | State the price or offer explicitly. Pair with the benefit — never let price stand alone. | "Get All 6 Volumes for Just $1 — Plus a 30-Day Free Trial" |

**Templates:**

```
How to [get specific desired result] without [main objection/obstacle]
Announcing: [new thing] that [does specific benefit] for [target person]
Do you make these [N] mistakes in [activity]?
Now: [end result the reader wants] — even if [limiting belief about themselves]
Why [counterintuitive claim] — and what to do instead
[N] reasons [claim that seems hard to believe]
"[Customer quote stating specific result]" — [attribution]
Get [specific thing] Free — just [low-friction action]
[Command verb] your [problem] in [timeframe]
```

**Watch out:**
- Pure curiosity without a benefit — readers are amused but don't act
- Vague benefit ("improve your life") instead of a specific result ("cut your heating bill 38%")
- Leading with the product name or company rather than the reader's payoff
- Writing a clever or punny headline that obscures the offer
- Using the curiosity category as a default instead of as a complement to self-interest

### The 4 U's of Strong Headlines — Michael Masterson (AWAI)
*Score any headline 1–4 on four dimensions — Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific — and rewrite until each score is a 3 or 4.*

**When:** When auditing or improving an existing headline, email subject line, or blog title — especially when it feels flat and you can't identify why. Also useful as a pre-write checklist.

**Components:**

- **Useful** *(most important)* — Answers "what's in it for me?" — shows a concrete benefit, solves a problem, or answers a question the reader is already asking. Without it, the others cannot compensate. Ask: does this headline promise the reader will gain something specific, solve something real, or learn something actionable? If the answer requires inference, rewrite with the benefit explicit. *Example: "How to Save $400 a Year on Car Insurance" — directly useful; the reader knows the exact payoff.*

- **Urgent** — Creates time pressure or immediacy — either external (deadline, limited offer) or internal (a pain/desire the reader feels right now). Add a timeframe, deadline, "before X happens", or tap a pain the reader is experiencing at this moment. Avoid manufactured urgency — root it in a real condition. *Example: "File Before April 15 and Claim These 7 Overlooked Deductions."*

- **Unique** — Offers a surprising angle, unexpected fact, or distinctive slant that separates this from every other headline on the same topic. Ask: could this headline appear on a competitor's ad? If yes, it's not unique. Add your mechanism, a counterintuitive claim, or a proprietary angle that belongs only to this offer. *Example: "The 3pm Energy Crash Is Not About Sleep — Here's the Real Cause."*

- **Ultra-Specific** — Replaces vague generalities with numbers, named steps, a precise timeframe, or an identifiable audience. Specificity signals credibility — it implies the writer actually did the work. Replace adjectives with quantities ("significantly" → "47%"), replace abstract nouns with named things ("your finances" → "$1,200 in medical bills"), name the exact reader when possible. *Example: "Lose 12 Pounds in 30 Days Without Giving Up Pasta" vs. "Lose Weight Fast."*

Priority when tradeoffs are needed: **Useful > Urgent > Unique > Ultra-specific**.

**Templates:**

```
How to [ultra-specific result] in [specific timeframe] — even if [named objection]
[Specific number] [specific thing] every [named audience] must know before [deadline]
The [counterintuitive claim] about [familiar topic] — and what to do by [date]
Why [specific group] [do/don't] [specific thing] — and the [number]-step fix
[Result] in [timeframe] using [named mechanism] (no [common cost/sacrifice] required)
```

**Watch out:**
- Trying to hit all 4 U's at maximum — the result is bloated; apply the priority order when tradeoffs are needed
- Manufactured urgency ("limited time only" on evergreen content) — readers recognize and discount it
- Confusing "unique angle" with "obscure angle" — uniqueness must still be relevant to the reader's actual concern
- Using specifics that don't build credibility ("7 amazing tips") — the number must connect to something verifiable
- Applying the 4 U's as a word-count formula instead of a relevance filter

### Ogilvy's Headline Rules — David Ogilvy
*Five times as many people read the headline as the body — so the headline must do the full selling job: name the brand, promise the benefit, and be specific enough to be believed.*

**When:** When writing any paid ad headline, advertorial title, or any context where most readers will not proceed past the first sentence. Critical check before finalizing any print, display, or email campaign.

**Components:**

- **Headline Primacy (the 5x rule)** — On average, 5x more people read the headline than the body copy. Ogilvy: "If you haven't done some selling in your headline, you have wasted 80 percent of your client's money." Write the headline last, after you know the single strongest thing you can say. Ask: if a reader sees only this one line, do they know what's being sold and why they should want it? *Example: "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock" — names the brand, promises quality, gives specific proof.*

- **Include the Brand Name** — Ogilvy: "If you don't, 80 percent of readers (who don't read your body copy) will never know what product you are advertising." Place the brand name in the headline unless it appears in an adjacent image unavoidably read with the headline. *Example: "Listerine Stops Halitosis" — brand name + specific benefit claim, four words.*

- **Promise a Specific Benefit (or Deliver News)** — Ogilvy: "Headlines that promise to benefit sell more than those that don't." Valid headline jobs: promise a benefit, deliver news, offer a service, tell a significant story, recognize a problem, or quote a satisfied customer. Generic boast ("We're the best") fails all of these. Translate features to outcomes: "12 megapixels" → "photos so sharp you can print them poster-size." *Example: "How Procter & Gamble Turned a Flaw into a Fortune."*

- **Specifics Over Generalities** — Ogilvy: "Specifics are more persuasive and memorable than generalities." A precise number, named place, concrete comparison, or verifiable claim is inherently more credible than vague superlatives. Audit every adjective and adverb: replace "great savings" with the dollar figure, "fast results" with the timeframe, "most people" with the percentage. *Example: "Only 1 in 4 financial advisors beats the S&P 500 over 20 years — here's how to find one who does" vs. "Get Better Investment Advice."*

- **Flag the Audience** — When a product serves a specific group, naming that group in the headline filters out non-buyers and dramatically increases relevance for the target. Insert the target descriptor at the front. *Example: "To men who want to quit work someday" (classic Merrill Lynch ad Ogilvy cited).*

- **Avoid Cleverness, Puns, and Obscurity** — Ogilvy: "Your headline should telegraph what you want to say — in simple language. Readers do not stop to decipher the meanings of obscure headlines." Sacrifice wit for specificity every time. *Bad: "Sole Mates" (shoe ad). Good: "Finally — a running shoe designed for women's feet, not men's."*

**Templates:**

```
[Brand name]: How to [specific desired result] in [specific timeframe]
Announcing [Brand]: the [specific thing] that [does benefit] for [named audience]
To [named audience] who want to [goal]: [specific claim or offer]
[Brand] [specific result claim] — here's why [credibility hook]
"[Customer quote with specific result]" — [Brand]
Why [specific group] choose [Brand] over [alternative]
```

**Watch out:**
- Omitting the brand name from the headline when body copy may not be read
- Writing a "creative" headline that entertains but doesn't identify the offer
- Using superlatives ("best," "greatest," "most powerful") without specific proof
- Targeting everyone — unflagged headlines pull less response than audience-specific ones
- Confusing Ogilvy's "news" rule with PR-style announcements — the news must be relevant to the reader's self-interest, not just company news

### Fascination Bullets (Curiosity Gaps) — Mel Martin; systematized by AWAI and Gary Bencivenga
*Each bullet opens a specific knowledge gap — naming the benefit without revealing the mechanism — forcing the reader to continue or buy to close it.*

**When:** Sales letters, VSL scripts, landing pages, table-of-contents copy, email body bullets, and anywhere you need to convert a lukewarm reader. Especially powerful in info-product, newsletter, and health/finance direct mail.

**Bullet types:**

- **Blind Bullet (pure curiosity gap)** — Names the benefit or outcome without revealing how. State the desired result or named danger; withhold the "how" or "what" entirely. *Example: "The one thing you should never say to your doctor before surgery."*

- **Half-Blind Bullet (partial reveal)** — Reveals the category or ingredient; withholds the application or dose. Use an em-dash or ellipsis to signal the withheld piece. Creates curiosity from inside a frame of trust. *Example: "Cinnamon manages blood sugar — but here's the exact form and amount that actually works."*

- **Giveaway Bullet (full value + hook for more)** — Delivers a real, standalone useful fact. Builds trust; demonstrates that what follows is worth having. Drives more sales than pure blind bullets when the product's value needs demonstrating. State a specific, useful, surprising fact complete enough to use, then position it as the tip of a larger iceberg. *Example: "The surprising tax deduction most homeowners miss: your PMI payments may be fully deductible through 2025."*

- **Warning / Alert Bullet** — Triggers loss-aversion. Open with "Warning:", "Never", "The one mistake", or "What your [authority] won't tell you." Name the specific harm without revealing the solution. *Example: "Warning: the one compliance mistake that gets health supplement ads banned overnight."*

- **Counterintuitive / Myth-bust Bullet** — Contradicts a belief the reader holds; forces reappraisal. Mel Martin's "Right? Wrong." format. Structure: "[Widely held belief]? Wrong. [Brief tease of the real answer]." *Example: "All aspirin is the same — right? Wrong. The brand that makes a critical difference on page 42."*

- **Secret / Insider Bullet** — Positions the withheld information as knowledge held by an authority the reader has been excluded from. Use "What [expert group] knows that you don't," "The insider secret," "What [authority] never tells you." *Example: "What IRS auditors look for first — and why most accountants never warn their clients."*

- **Number / List Bullet** — A specific numbered claim creates implicit credibility. Use odd numbers over round numbers when available ("17" reads more credible than "20"). Lead with the number, then name the category with enough specificity to feel exclusive. *Example: "The 7 questions a smart buyer asks before signing any real estate contract."*

**Templates:**

```
The one [thing/mistake/secret] you should never [do/say/ignore] when [situation]
[Common belief]? Wrong. The [specific correction] on page [X]
[Category]: the [surprising/overlooked thing] that [specific result]
Warning: the [specific error] that [specific bad outcome] — and how to avoid it
What every [expert/insider group] knows about [topic] — and why they rarely share it
[Specific number] [things/questions/mistakes] every [named audience] must know before [action]
[Ingredient/method] [widely known benefit] — but here's the [form/dose/timing] that actually works
How to [goal] — without [main sacrifice/cost the reader fears]
```

**Watch out:**
- Teasing without payoff — if the product doesn't deliver the promised secret, the reader feels cheated and refunds/unsubscribes
- Using too many blind bullets in a row — reader fatigue; mix blind, giveaway, and warning types to maintain credibility
- Vague danger ("the mistake that costs you money") instead of specific danger ("the IRS trigger word that invites an audit")
- Stacking fascinations on unrelated benefits — all bullets should point toward the same core desire
- Recycling obvious "secrets" the reader already knows — destroys the illusion of insider access immediately

## 3. Credibility (make every claim believable)

### Scientific Advertising Copy-Credibility System — Claude C. Hopkins
*Six interlocking rules that make advertising measurable, credible, and self-funding by treating every ad as a salesman whose cost per sale can be tracked.*

**When:** Writing direct-response copy, landing pages, product ads, or any sales message where the claim could be dismissed as puffery — especially when competing against louder, vaguer rivals.

**Components:**

- **Reason-Why Copy** — Every claim must be backed by an explicit reason the reader can evaluate. Hopkins adopted this principle from John E. Kennedy (1904): advertising is "salesmanship in print," and a salesman who cannot say why is a bad salesman. For each benefit claim, ask: "Why is this true?" Write the answer as the next sentence. Strip every adjective with no supporting mechanism. If you cannot complete the sentence "because ___", the claim is puffery — cut it or find the proof. *Example — Weak: "Our coffee is richer and more satisfying." Strong: "We cold-brew for 20 hours at 4°C, which extracts twice the antioxidants and none of the bitter acids that hot water releases — that is why the finish is smooth, never sharp."*

- **Preemptive Claim** — Find a true, verifiable fact about your product that the entire category shares but no competitor has bothered to say publicly. Claim it first, in print, and own the association. Hopkins did this for Schlitz beer: every brewery steam-cleaned its bottles, but Schlitz was the first to say so — any competitor who later claimed it looked like a copycat. The principle: truth + timing + silence of rivals = exclusive positioning. List 10 mundane production or ingredient facts; check which rival has stated each one publicly; pick the fact with the highest perceived value that zero rivals have claimed; build your headline around it. Do not imply it is unique — simply state it vividly and first. *Example: "Our capsules are nitrogen-flushed before sealing — the same process pharmaceutical labs use to prevent oxidation." (A category-wide practice, stated first.)*

- **Specificity Over Superlatives** — Exact numbers outperform evaluative adjectives because they imply measurement has been done. Hopkins: "Softens the beard in one minute" vs. "Acts quickly." "Three and one-third times brighter" beats "much brighter." Hopkins: "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck. Specificities make people realize that tests and comparisons have been made." Odd, non-round numbers are more credible because round numbers feel estimated. Audit every adjective and adverb; find its numeric equivalent. *Example — Before: "Our blades stay sharper longer than competitors." After: "Our blades stay sharp for 17 shaves on average — tested against the five best-selling brands, which dulled by shave 9."*

- **Salesmanship in Print** — An ad is a salesman multiplied across thousands of readers. Judge it by the same standard: does it identify the prospect, establish need, present a credible solution, handle objections, and close? Hopkins: "The only purpose of advertising is to make sales. It is profitable or unprofitable according to its actual sales." Read your copy aloud as if you were a door-to-door salesman. Mark every line a good salesman would never say ("world-class," "industry-leading," "passionate about"). Replace those lines with the next objection the prospect would voice, and answer it. Ensure the copy ends with a single, clear action request. *Example: Instead of "Revolutionizing the way you sleep," write what a skilled mattress salesman would say: "If you wake up stiff three days a week, the problem is almost certainly your lumbar support — here is why this layer matters and how to check yours in 60 seconds."*

- **Sampling and the Qualified Offer** — Let the product sell itself by getting it into qualified hands — but require a small act of engagement first. Hopkins found free samples with zero friction attracted unqualified takers who never converted. Requiring a coupon or reply card pre-qualifies interest. His rule: "An article good enough for the maker to buy for the user is good enough for the user to buy." Do not call the offer "free" — call it a trial, a sample sent at the maker's cost, or a risk-free test. Require one small step (clip a coupon, fill a form, pay shipping). Frame the offer as: "We will send you X — at our expense — because we know once you try it, you will understand why [specific claim]."

- **Keyed Measurement Before Scaling** — Never scale spend on an unverified ad. Assign a unique key (coupon code, department number, URL) to each ad version and each publication. Count responses and calculate cost-per-customer, not impressions. Hopkins: "We compare one way with many others, backward and forward, and record the results." A headline change alone can multiply returns 5–10×. This is the 1923 origin of A/B testing. Create 2–3 variants differing on exactly one variable (headline, offer, or lead paragraph); run simultaneously to comparable audiences; measure cost-per-conversion; kill losing variants; scale only the winner.

**Templates:**

```
REASON-WHY:
[Benefit] — because [specific mechanism or ingredient or process that produces it].
Example: "No bitter aftertaste — because we remove the coffee oil compounds at 34°F before they oxidize."

PREEMPTIVE CLAIM:
[Category-wide true fact stated vividly] — and we are the first to make this commitment visible because [what it means for the user].
Example: "Every capsule is nitrogen-flushed before sealing — the same step pharmaceutical labs use to block oxidation — because potency at the moment you take it is the only potency that counts."

SPECIFICITY SWAP:
Replace [vague adjective] with [number + unit + test condition].
Before: "Heals faster." After: "Visible improvement in 48 hours — confirmed in a 200-person patch test."

SALESMANSHIP CLOSE:
"If [specific pain], here is the one thing worth knowing: [mechanism]. That is why [product] does [outcome]. [Single clear CTA]."

QUALIFIED OFFER:
"We will send you [sample/trial] — at our cost — because once you [specific sensory or functional experience], you will understand why [specific claim]. [CTA to claim it]."
```

**Watch out:**
- Stating the preemptive claim but hedging it with "like all quality brands" — this cancels the positioning; state the fact plainly without apology
- Using a specific number without a real test behind it — readers sense confabulated precision
- Offering a free sample with zero friction (pop-up "click to claim") — Hopkins explicitly found this attracts unqualified takers who never convert; require one qualifying step
- Treating "salesmanship in print" as license to be verbose — one claim, fully proven, outperforms five claims stated without proof
- Running a single ad variant and calling the result a test — a test with no control tells you only that the ad ran
- Measuring click-through rate or impressions instead of cost-per-customer — always trace to a sale or a qualified action
- Applying specificity cosmetically: writing "47% more effective" without a comparator, methodology, or timeframe — this is a superlative in numerical disguise

## 4. Structures (proven copy skeletons)

For Cialdini's principles (Reciprocity, Social Proof, Authority, Scarcity, etc.) that layer into any of these structures, see [persuasion.md](persuasion.md). For matching offer enhancers — guarantees, bonuses, scarcity mechanisms — see [offers.md](offers.md).

### AIDA — E. St. Elmo Lewis; acronym: C.P. Russell, 1921
*Move a cold reader from first noticing your message to taking a specific action by walking them through four escalating mental states.*

**When:** Any single-channel ad, landing page headline stack, or email where the reader starts cold and must be converted in one pass — display ads, Google ads, homepage hero sections, cold outreach subject + body.

- **Attention** — Stop the scroll or scan. The reader has given you nothing yet; you must earn the first second. Write a headline or opening line that names the reader's exact situation, a surprising fact, or a bold claim. No preamble, no warmup. Cut until only the hook remains. *Example: "Accountants who bill by the hour are charging you for their learning curve."*

- **Interest** — Sustain focus by connecting your topic to something the reader already cares about. Introduce a specific problem, tension, or curiosity gap relevant to them. Use data, a short story, or a contrast. This is where you earn the right to make a claim. *Example: "The average small business overpays $4,200/year in accounting fees — not because of complex filings, but because most accountants re-learn your business every April."*

- **Desire** — Make the reader want your specific outcome, not just acknowledge the problem. Show the after-state in concrete, sensory terms. Use testimonials, visuals, or a "day in the life" description. Shift from "here is the problem" to "here is what your life looks like with it solved." *Example: "Our clients close their books in 3 hours every month — not 3 days. One client used the freed time to launch a product line that now outearns his core business."*

- **Action** — Tell the reader exactly what to do next, right now. Give a single, low-friction CTA. Name what happens when they click/call. Remove or answer any residual objection in the same breath. *Example: "Book a 20-minute call — we'll review your last invoice and show you exactly where the overcharges are. No commitment. [Find a time]"*

**Templates:**

```
[Attention — bold claim or named situation]: [one sentence]
[Interest — relevant problem with specific evidence]: [2-3 sentences]
[Desire — vivid after-state or proof]: [2-3 sentences]
[Action — single CTA + friction remover]: [one sentence]

Headline: [Surprising fact or direct call-out of reader's situation]
Subhead: [Connect fact to reader's specific pain with a number]
Body: [Show the outcome one customer achieved]
CTA: [Verb + what they get + no-risk qualifier]
```

**Watch out:**
- Skipping Attention and opening with company name or logo — reader never gets in
- Conflating Interest and Desire — Interest builds curiosity, Desire builds want; collapsing them produces vague enthusiasm that converts nobody
- Weak Action: "Learn more" or "Contact us" with no specificity about what happens next
- Writing Desire as a feature list instead of an experienced outcome
- Using AIDA for retention or upsell copy (wrong context — reader is not cold)

### PAS — Dan Kennedy
*Identify a pain, intensify it until the reader feels it physically, then position your solution as the only logical exit.*

**When:** Short-form copy where the reader is already vaguely aware of the problem but has not yet acted: Facebook/Instagram ads, email subject + opening, short sales page sections, cold email openers. Best when emotional stakes are high.

- **Problem** — Name the specific pain the reader is living with. Not a category — the exact, felt version. State it in the reader's own words, not marketing language. Mirror the internal monologue. Be specific enough that the right reader says "that's me" and the wrong reader tunes out. *Example: "You're posting on LinkedIn every day and getting crickets."*

- **Agitate** — Press on the pain until the cost of inaction is viscerally clear. Do not move to solution yet. List downstream consequences: financial, emotional, social. Ask rhetorical questions that make the reader confront what they've been avoiding. This is the longest part of PAS copy — it builds the pressure that makes the solution feel like relief. *Example: "Meanwhile, your competitor who posts half as often just landed the contract you pitched for. Every day you spend invisible on LinkedIn is a day someone else is becoming the obvious choice in your niche. Prospects are Googling you right now and finding nothing."*

- **Solution** — Present your offer as the specific exit from the pain you have just made undeniable. Introduce the solution in direct contrast to the agitated pain. Name the mechanism (what specifically makes it work), then CTA. *Example: "LinkedIn Authority Sprint is a 6-week program that builds your content engine once, so posts go out consistently without you writing from scratch each week. 90 minutes of your time total. [See how it works]"*

**Templates:**

```
[Problem — exact pain in reader's language]: [1 sentence]
[Agitate — 3-5 sentences on cost, consequences, compounding]: [paragraph]
[Solution — mechanism + CTA]: [2-3 sentences]

You know that feeling when [specific frustrating situation]?
[Agitate: what it's costing them, what they're missing, how it compounds.]
[Solution name] fixes this by [specific mechanism]. [CTA].
```

**Watch out:**
- Agitating a problem the reader does not yet believe they have — establish the problem as real before twisting the knife
- Under-agitating: one sentence of problem, one of agitation, jump to solution — the formula only works when Agitate is genuinely uncomfortable
- Over-agitating without relief — reader feels hopeless rather than ready to act; always land on solvable
- Using PAS on a warm, loyal audience — it reads as manipulative when the reader already trusts you
- Naming the solution before the agitation is complete — releases the tension prematurely

### BAB — origin unverified; widely attributed to Bob Bly (unconfirmed by primary sources)
*Show readers exactly where they are now, then show them exactly where they could be, then explain that your product is the bridge between those two states.*

**When:** Email subject + opening lines, social media ads, short video scripts, and any copy where transformation is the core promise. Works best when the "after" state is highly desirable and the gap between Before and After is vivid.

> ⚠️ "Bob Bly created BAB" is repeated across practitioner sites but is not confirmed by Bly's own books or a dateable primary source. Treat attribution as unverified.

- **Before** — Describe the reader's current, unsatisfying reality in specific, recognizable terms. Paint the current state without exaggerating or manipulating — just make it concrete and relatable. Use specific details (numbers, daily frustrations, missed opportunities) so the reader nods. *Example: "Right now, you're spending Sunday evenings writing next week's emails, only to watch open rates sit at 18%."*

- **After** — Paint the desired future state with equal or greater specificity. Describe the outcome the reader actually wants — the emotion, the tangible result, the daily experience. Keep it anchored in reality; over-promising here destroys credibility at the Bridge stage. *Example: "Imagine your emails going out automatically, open rates climbing to 34%, and Sunday evenings free again."*

- **Bridge** — Introduce your product or approach as the mechanism that moves the reader from Before to After. Name what the solution is, briefly explain why it works (the mechanism), and give a CTA. The Bridge should feel inevitable given what you established in Before and After. *Example: "That's what EmailFlow does: it builds a behavior-triggered sequence once and sends the right message to the right subscriber automatically. [Start your free trial]"*

**Templates:**

```
Before: [Specific current pain or frustrating state in reader's language]
After: [Specific desired outcome — concrete, sensory, believable]
Bridge: [Your solution] makes this happen by [mechanism]. [CTA].

Right now, [problem in concrete terms].
Imagine instead: [outcome in concrete terms].
[Product] is how you get there. [CTA].
```

**Watch out:**
- Making the After state too generic ("imagine more freedom") — specificity is what makes it believable and desirable
- Burying the Bridge: spending 80% on Before/After and giving a vague one-liner as the Bridge — the Bridge must be credible, not just named
- Using BAB when the reader does not yet believe the After is achievable — need proof before the Bridge
- Confusing BAB with PAS: BAB is aspirational (pull toward the good), PAS is avoidance (push away from pain); mixing the emotional register weakens both

### FAB — emerged from sales training tradition (mid-20th century; no single verified originator)
*Prevent the reader from drowning in specs by translating each product feature into what it does (advantage) and what that means for the buyer's life (benefit).*

**When:** Product descriptions, sales decks, B2B proposal copy, spec-heavy landing pages, and any situation where there is a risk of feature-dumping. The operative question is always: "So what does that mean for the customer?"

- **Feature** — A factual attribute of the product or service — what it has or what it does technically. State it plainly and precisely. Never lead with the feature in customer-facing copy; it is only the foundation you build from. *Example: "Our project management tool has a real-time Gantt chart."*

- **Advantage** — What the feature does in operation — how it performs relative to the alternative. Bridge from the feature to its functional effect with "which means" or "so you can." The advantage is still about the product but in terms of what it enables. This is the transition step most copywriters skip. *Example: "...which automatically updates whenever a task is completed or delayed..."*

- **Benefit** — The personal outcome the buyer experiences as a result — the "what's in it for me." Translate the advantage into the reader's actual world: time, money, status, emotion, risk reduction. Be specific. Use the reader's context, not a generic positive. This is the only part the reader truly buys. *Example: "...so your team leads always know exactly where the project stands without a single status meeting."*

**Templates:**

```
[Feature] — [Advantage: what it does in operation] — [Benefit: what that means for the buyer's life].

Full sentence: "[Product] has [feature], which means [advantage], so you can [benefit]."

[Feature]: [state it plainly]
What it does: [advantage — functional effect]
What it means for you: [benefit — personal outcome]
[CTA]
```

**Watch out:**
- Stopping at Feature: listing specs without any translation — the reader has to do the work you refused to do, and most won't
- Jumping from Feature to Benefit and skipping Advantage — the claim feels unearned without the mechanical step
- Writing Benefits that are still generic ("saves time," "increases productivity") — these are Advantage-level claims dressed as Benefits; go one step further to a specific, contextual outcome
- Applying FAB to every feature indiscriminately — only features that correspond to the buyer's top priorities deserve the full treatment
- Using FAB copy for emotional or identity-driven products (luxury, fashion, meaning) where the customer is not making a rational decision

### 4 Ps (Picture-Promise-Proof-Push) — Henry Hoke Sr.
*Open with a vivid scene the reader inhabits, make an explicit promise, back it with proof, then push to action — a complete persuasion arc in four moves.*

**When:** Long-form direct mail, sales pages, fundraising letters, and persuasive emails where you have enough space to build a full emotional and logical case. More structured than PAS; better for offers requiring both emotional engagement and rational justification.

- **Picture** — Create a vivid, concrete scene the reader can inhabit — either their current pain or their desired future. Open with sensory, specific description. Place the reader inside a moment (a frustrating morning, a triumphant outcome). Readers should see themselves in the scene before you say a word about your product. *Example: "It's 11pm. Your proposal is due tomorrow and you're still converting spreadsheets by hand — again."*

- **Promise** — State explicitly what your offer will deliver. Make one clear, falsifiable promise — the single headline outcome. Not a list of benefits. The Promise should directly resolve the tension from the Picture. *Example: "DataBridge eliminates manual data conversion entirely — any spreadsheet, any format, into your CRM in under 60 seconds."*

- **Proof** — Back your Promise with evidence the skeptical reader will accept. Choose proof that matches the reader's likely objection: if they doubt effectiveness, use case study numbers; if they doubt safety, use a guarantee or credentials; if they doubt relevance, use a customer who looks like them. One strong, specific proof beats five generic ones. *Example: "Acme Corp's operations team processed 2,400 records in 38 minutes — a task that previously took three people an entire workday. [Link to case study]"*

- **Push** — Remove the last friction and tell the reader exactly what to do — with urgency that is earned, not manufactured. State the CTA, give a real reason to act now (deadline, limited spots, price changing), and reduce perceived risk (free trial, money-back guarantee, cancel anytime). *Example: "Try DataBridge free for 14 days — no credit card required. Import your first file in under 5 minutes or we'll personally set it up for you. [Start free trial]"*

**Templates:**

```
[Picture — sensory scene of pain or desire]: [2-3 sentences]
[Promise — explicit single benefit claim]: [1-2 sentences]
[Proof — specific evidence matching the reader's top objection]: [2-3 sentences]
[Push — CTA + urgency reason + risk remover]: [1-2 sentences]

Scene: [Reader in a specific frustrating or aspirational moment]
Promise: [Product] means [concrete outcome] — [specific number or timeframe].
Proof: [Customer name] achieved [specific measurable result]. [Social proof link]
Act now: [CTA] — [risk remover].
```

**Watch out:**
- Opening with Promise instead of Picture: skipping the emotional setup produces a claim the reader has no reason to care about yet
- Proof that is too vague ("many customers love it") or too self-referential ("our team says it works") — proof only works when independently verifiable or strikingly specific
- Push that is dishonest: manufactured scarcity or fake deadlines; once caught, they destroy all the credibility built in the first three Ps
- Confusing Picture with an abstract statement of the problem — Picture must be sensory and specific, not a category description
- Note on ordering: some practitioners lead with Promise and put Picture second; both orderings are defensible, with Promise-first trading emotional warmth for immediate clarity

### Robert Collier's 6-Step Sales Letter — Robert Collier, *The Robert Collier Letter Book* (1931)
*Build a long-form sales letter as a momentum arc: open on the reader's inner conversation, escalate desire and reason, introduce urgency, and close with a single clear action.*

**When:** Long-form direct mail, sales letters, long-form sales pages, and fundraising appeals where you have the reader's time and the offer requires full persuasion. Collier's defining principle: "Enter the conversation already taking place in the prospect's mind."

- **The Opening** — Hook the reader by entering the conversation already happening in their head — not your conversation about your product. Start with the reader's existing thought, worry, desire, or question — stated in their language. Avoid starting with "I," your company name, or your product name. *Example: "You've probably wondered whether there's a way to retire 10 years earlier without gambling your savings on the market."*

- **The Description or Explanation** — Paint a vivid picture of the problem being solved and the product or offer that solves it. Introduce the product as the actor in a story of change — not as a spec list. Show what it does, for whom, with sensory detail. *Example: "The Collier Retirement Blueprint is a 12-week self-paced program built around one principle: cut the fees that silently drain 1.2% of your portfolio every year — fees most investors never see on a statement."*

- **The Motive or Reason Why** — Appeal to the reader's primary emotional driver — the real why beneath the surface want. Collier identified six prime human motives: **love, gain, duty, pride, self-indulgence, and self-preservation**. Identify which one drives your reader and make the appeal explicit. This is where you connect product benefit to emotional identity. *Example: "This isn't just about money. It's about being the parent who retires in time to travel with their kids before they're grown — not after."*

- **The Proof or Guarantee** — Remove doubt with credible, specific evidence. Provide testimonials with specific outcomes (not vague praise), case studies, data, credentials, or a guarantee that directly reverses the reader's largest fear. Specificity is what separates proof from assertion. *Example: "412 families have completed the program. Average fee reduction in year one: $3,800. If you don't identify at least $1,000 in hidden fees in the first module, email us and we'll refund every cent."*

- **The Snapper or Penalty** — Create genuine urgency by making the cost of delay real and concrete. Do not manufacture fake scarcity. Calculate and state the real cost of waiting: money lost per month, opportunity missed, compounding disadvantage. *Example: "Every month you delay, those 1.2% fees compound. On a $300,000 portfolio, that's $300 gone this month alone — not to your future, to your fund manager's."*

- **The Close** — State the desired action once, clearly and specifically, making it as easy as possible to take. Give a single CTA. Repeat the key benefit in one line. Name exactly what happens when they respond. Remove any remaining friction. Do not add new selling points — the sale is already made; this is just the mechanism. *Example: "Click below to enroll. You'll get immediate access to Module 1 — a 22-minute video that shows you where to find the fees on your current statements. If it doesn't pay for itself in the first week, we'll refund you in full. [Enroll now — $97]"*

**Templates:**

```
Opening: [Reader's internal question or worry, in their words]
Description: [Product as the actor in a story of change — what it does, for whom]
Motive: [Connect the benefit to a primary emotional driver: love / gain / duty / pride / self-indulgence / self-preservation]
Proof: [Specific testimonial with numbers or a concrete guarantee that reverses the main fear]
Snapper: [Calculate the real monthly cost of inaction — don't fabricate urgency]
Close: [Single CTA + immediate benefit + friction remover]
```

**Watch out:**
- Opening on yourself or your product instead of the reader's existing internal monologue — Collier's entire system hinges on entering the conversation already in progress
- Skipping the Motive step and treating the letter as a rational persuasion exercise — the emotional driver is what transforms interested readers into buyers
- Using the Snapper with manufactured or implausible urgency ("only 3 spots left" when selling a digital download) — destroys credibility built across the earlier steps
- A Close that adds new information or a new argument — if you haven't made the case by the Close, another bullet point won't save you
- Writing the letter from your company's perspective rather than the reader's — every sentence should serve the reader's needs

### Sugarman's Slippery Slide — Joseph Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook* (2007)
*Design every element of an ad so its only job is to make the reader read the next element — creating frictionless forward momentum from headline to purchase.*

**When:** Any long-form copy where reader drop-off before the CTA is the main conversion problem: long sales pages, email sequences, long-form Facebook ads, direct mail letters. Also use as a diagnostic applied to any copy structure — read each sentence and ask "does this earn the next one?"

- **Headline** — Its only job: get the reader to read the subheadline. Not to sell, not to explain — only to compel the next eye movement. Make it curious, specific, or disruptive enough that stopping feels unfinished. Sugarman: the headline's job is "to get the reader to read the first sentence." *Example: "The Sunglasses That Killed My Reading Glasses."*

- **Deck Copy / Subheadline** — Expands the headline just enough to reward the reader for pausing. Introduce the central benefit or tension in 1-2 lines. Do not resolve the curiosity — deepen it. *Example: "How BluBlocker lenses accidentally solved a problem optometrists have been ignoring for 30 years."*

- **First Sentence** — The entry point to the slide. Its job is to get the second sentence read. Make it short (5-10 words), easy to read, syntactically incomplete or forward-leaning. No polysyllabic words. No claims yet — just a frictionless first step. Sugarman: "It should be so easy to read that it compels you to read the second." *Example: "It started with a headache."*

- **Body Copy Progression** — Each sentence's only job is to get the next sentence read. Momentum is the product. Use open loops, incomplete thoughts, questions that require reading on, narrative tension that hasn't resolved. Vary sentence length — short punches break flow, medium sentences carry argument, longer sentences immerse. Cut any sentence that does not earn the next one. *Example: "I'd worn glasses for eleven years. Tried every lens. Nothing helped on the water — until a customer returned a pair of BluBlockers by mistake and I put them on."*

- **Credibility and Proof** — Where the reader's logical brain needs to catch up with the forward momentum. Insert proof at the natural moment of skepticism — usually after the core benefit has been established and before the price/CTA. Use specific numbers, named customers, demonstrations, or a personal story with verifiable details. This is the "rational justification for an emotional decision." *Example: "We sold 180,000 pairs in our first year. Not by advertising the fashion — by letting customers report what they saw differently."*

- **Close and CTA** — The bottom of the slide — the natural landing point after frictionless descent. State the offer, price, and CTA without introducing new concepts. Add a guarantee to eliminate the last hesitation. Make the physical action as simple as possible. *Example: "BluBlockers: $29.95. 30-day money-back guarantee — no questions. Call 1-800-XXX-XXXX or order at [URL]. If they don't change what you see, send them back."*

**Templates:**

```
Headline: [Curiosity or surprising claim — job: get first line read]
Deck: [1-2 lines deepening the tension — job: get reader to commit to body]
First sentence: [5-10 words, frictionless, forward-leaning]
Body: [Chain of sentences, each earning the next: open loop → partial resolution → new question → story → proof → offer build-up]
Close: [Offer + price + guarantee + single CTA]

Diagnostic pass:
Read your copy aloud. After each sentence, ask: "Does this sentence make me want to read the next one?"
If the answer is no, cut or rewrite until it does.
```

**Watch out:**
- Front-loading benefits in the headline — when the headline resolves the tension, there is no reason to read the subhead
- A first sentence that is long, complex, or packed with claims — stops momentum before it starts
- Proof inserted too early (before the benefit is established) — reads as defensive rather than confirmatory
- Paragraphs that could be removed without the reader noticing a gap — every block must advance the chain
- Treating the Slippery Slide as a structure to fill rather than a test to apply — the real use is diagnostic

### The Live-Commerce Close — Chinese livestream-selling convention (直播带货)
*Handle the buyer's four objection-classes in a fixed order during a live, synchronous pitch: build the worth (价值), anchor it against the alternative (对比), remove the risk (安全感), then give a real reason to act now (稀缺/紧迫).*

**When:** Real-time, synchronous selling where you must move a watching audience to buy in one continuous pass and answer objections live — shopping livestreams, product demos, webinars, sales calls, launch videos, and demo-driven landing pages. This is the live counterpart to the written skeletons above (AIDA, PAS, Collier): the same persuasion, sequenced for a room watching you rather than a reader going at their own pace.

> ⚠️ This sequence circulates widely in Chinese live-commerce training under many instructors' names with no single verifiable originator — kept here as a field-tested **convention, not an authority**. Its value is that each of its four moves *is* one of the verified frameworks below, re-ordered for live delivery — not a new mechanism. Treat unverified guru attributions ("X laoshi's secret system") with the skepticism the rest of this skill applies.

- **1. Value (价值 — build the worth)** — Establish the dream outcome and *demonstrate* the product delivering it before any price, comparison, or close. Live selling's one edge over copy is that you can show, not claim — so the demonstration itself is the value. Map: Value Equation + Mass Desire. *Apply:* name the outcome in the buyer's own words; run the result on camera in the first 30 seconds; stack quick, specific proof as you go. *e.g.* a kitchen stream sears the steak in the pan live before mentioning the price.

- **2. Comparison (对比 — anchor against the alternative)** — Position against what the buyer would otherwise do or buy, on the single dimension they care most about, and set a higher price reference before you reveal yours. Map: the Competitive Alternative ([positioning.md](positioning.md)) + price anchoring + Hopkins' preemptive claim (§3). *Apply:* one honest head-to-head on the buyer's top criterion; state the high anchor first, then your price; contrast on a dimension you genuinely win — never disparage. *e.g.* "in store this is ¥899; here it's less, and here's why we can do that."

- **3. Safety (安全感 — remove the risk)** — Dissolve "will it work for *me*, and what if it doesn't" with proof, guarantee, and trust signals — the toll gate every sale passes through. Map: the Guarantee Taxonomy ([offers.md](offers.md)) + Cialdini Authority & Social Proof ([persuasion.md](persuasion.md)) + Bencivenga's proof (§5). *Apply:* state the risk-reversal plainly; show proof from the buyer's *exact* cohort, not the most impressive one; name the escape hatch ("return it any time / you can always fall back to…"). *e.g.* "30-day no-questions return — try it, then decide."

- **4. Scarcity / Deficit (稀缺 · 紧迫 — why now)** — Give a *real* reason to act now, or a watching buyer defers forever. Map: Cialdini Scarcity ([persuasion.md](persuasion.md)) + Hormozi Scarcity/Urgency ([offers.md](offers.md)) + Collier's Snapper (§4). *Apply:* state the genuine limit *and its reason* (real stock, real deadline, real cohort cap). Where no supply limit honestly exists, switch to loss-framing the status quo — the compounding cost of *not* acting — rather than inventing one. *e.g.* "200 in this batch, 160 already gone" (if true), or "every week you wait is another week paying the old way."

**Templates:**

```
Live-close run sheet (say them in this order):
1. Value:      "Watch what this does — [demonstrate the outcome now]. That means [dream outcome] for you."
2. Comparison: "The usual way is [alternative] at [high anchor]. This [wins on their top criterion] — for [your price]."
3. Safety:     "You're covered: [guarantee]. People exactly like you: [cohort proof]. Worst case: [escape hatch]."
4. Scarcity:   "There's [real limit / deadline], so now is the moment — [single CTA]."
               (No real limit? → "Every [period] you wait costs you [concrete loss].")
```

**Watch out:**
- **The order is load-bearing.** Scarcity before Value is pressure with nothing behind it; Comparison before Value argues before they want anything. Earn the want first.
- **It's a consumer-impulse cadence.** For skeptical, research-driven buyers (developers, technical B2B, high-ticket), *expand* Safety (proof, correctness, no-lock-in) and *soften* Scarcity to honest loss-framing — the hard close reads as manipulation and repels them.
- **Every move must be real.** If you can't fill Safety with a true guarantee/proof, or Scarcity with a true limit, the close is hollow — fix the offer, don't fake the move.
- **Fake scarcity burns the trust** the first three moves just built (see the Snapper and Cialdini Scarcity warnings).

## 5. The pitch & the diagnostic

### Bencivenga Persuasion Equation — Gary Bencivenga
*A four-part diagnostic that forces every ad to earn belief before asking for the sale, by stacking proof against every claim.*

**When:** Writing any sales letter, long-form ad, VSL, or landing page — especially for skeptical markets or big claims. Use as an audit checklist before launch: if any of the four Ps is weak, the ad will fail at that hinge.

- **Urgent Problem** — Lead with a problem the prospect is actively trying to solve today — not a latent wish. Urgency is the ignition: if they're not already in pain, your copy has to create it before the promise lands. Open the headline or lead with the problem in the prospect's own words. Ask: is this problem screaming for their attention right now, or is it just mildly annoying? If the latter, amplify the consequence of ignoring it. *Example: "The Social Security time bomb most retirees won't see until it wipes out 40% of their monthly check" — surfaces a real, urgent fear rather than a generic "retirement planning" angle.*

- **Unique Promise** — The mechanism that solves the problem must be new or exclusive — not just a different brand of the same solution. The uniqueness justifies reading on and blocks direct price comparison. Articulate the "new mechanism": what specifically is different about how your product produces the result? Avoid category claims ("the best supplement"); name the proprietary mechanism. Run the "so what" test: could a competitor say the same thing? If yes, dig deeper. *Example: Instead of "lose weight fast," the promise becomes "resets your liver's fat-burning switch that most diets accidentally shut off" — mechanism makes it unique and non-copyable.*

- **Unquestionable Proof** — Proof is the engine of the equation — Bencivenga considered it the single most neglected element. Every meaningful claim must be matched with evidence that removes doubt. Proof types are cumulative: more independent sources = higher belief. After each major claim, apply Bencivenga's "Yeah, sure…" test: could a skeptical reader dismiss this? If yes, add: specific measurable results in testimonials (numbers, time frames, names), clinical or third-party studies, expert endorsements, or a risk-reversal guarantee. Proof should appear early — not just in a testimonial section at the end. *Example: Bencivenga's own agency ad: "I have created more winning controls in the most competitive markets than any other copywriter" — immediately followed by named clients (Phillips Publishing, Rodale Press, Boardroom), specific controls that ran for years, and client names who could verify.*

- **User-friendly Proposition** — Even a convinced buyer will not act if the next step is confusing, risky, or inconvenient. State one single next action. Reverse the risk (money-back guarantee, free trial, keep-the-bonus-regardless). Remove cognitive load from the decision: tell them exactly what arrives, when, how, and what to do if dissatisfied. Apply the "drunk user" standard — would someone skimming understand what to do next? *Example: Boardroom's Bottom Line Personal: "30-day free trial — if you don't agree it's the most valuable newsletter you've ever read, return the invoice marked CANCEL and owe nothing. The first issue is yours to keep." One action, zero risk, clear fallback.*

**Templates:**

```
Urgent Problem:
[Specific fear/pain] is [consequence] for [audience] who [triggering situation] — and most don't realize it until [too-late moment].

Unique Promise:
The only [product category] that [mechanism that is different] — without [common objection or sacrifice].

Proof block:
[Name, title, city] achieved [specific measurable result] in [time frame] using [product]. [One sentence quote in their words.]

Proposition:
To claim your [offer], [single verb action] below. Your order is backed by a [X]-day [guarantee]. If [dissatisfied condition], [exact remedy] — no questions asked.
```

**Watch out:**
- Writing the promise first and bolting proof on at the end as a testimonial appendix — Bencivenga said proof must be woven in as early as the second paragraph
- Using vague social proof ("thousands of satisfied customers") instead of specific measurable proof (names, numbers, time frames)
- Neglecting the "Urgent" qualifier — a real problem that isn't urgent today produces a "maybe later" response
- Treating the proposition as a formality ("order now") instead of a friction-removal exercise
- Claiming a "unique" promise that any competitor can make ("the most comprehensive system") — uniqueness requires a named mechanism

### Hook-Story-Offer — Russell Brunson
*A three-part content structure for any individual piece of marketing — ad, email, video script — that transfers belief before presenting the offer by letting a story do the persuasion.*

**When:** Writing any individual piece of marketing content where a cold or warm prospect needs to be moved from attention to offer acceptance. Pairs with the Value Ladder (which own funnels/distribution) and [distribution.md](distribution.md) for platform strategy.

- **Hook** — A pattern interrupt that stops the target's current mental activity and directs attention to your message. The hook creates a micro-commitment: "I will spend the next 30 seconds on this." State one counterintuitive truth, one bold specific claim, or one image/phrase that creates curiosity or tension. Test multiple hooks against the same audience with paid traffic before scaling — Brunson tests 20+ hooks on cold audiences. The hook must be platform-native: what stops a scroll on Facebook differs from what stops a scroll on LinkedIn. *Example: "What if everything you know about funnels is killing your conversions?" — interrupts a marketer's pattern, promises a reversal of existing belief, creates curiosity. Specific enough to filter for the right audience, broad enough to capture a large segment.*

- **Story (The Epiphany Bridge)** — A personal narrative that recreates the emotional experience of the moment you discovered the solution, so the prospect experiences the same "aha" themselves rather than being told about it. Stories transfer belief without triggering sales resistance. Follow the **Epiphany Bridge script**: (1) The backstory — who you were before, including the struggle. (2) The external journey — what you tried that failed. (3) The wall — the moment of maximum desperation. (4) The epiphany — the new belief or mechanism you discovered. (5) The results — what happened after. The story must demonstrate the new belief the prospect needs to hold before the offer lands. *Example: Brunson's "potato gun story" — describes failing at his first business, finding a sales funnel structure by accident while selling potato gun plans, recognizing the pattern. By the end, the prospect believes "funnels work and this person figured them out" — the exact belief needed before the ClickFunnels offer.*

- **Offer** — Everything the prospect receives — the product, the bonuses, the guarantee, the price — assembled to make the value self-evident. The offer is not the product; it is the product plus everything that removes the remaining objections to buying. Stack value by listing each component separately with its standalone value. Add bonuses that each address a specific remaining objection. Add a guarantee that eliminates financial risk. Present the stacked value total, then reveal the actual price. The ratio of value to price should feel irrationally favorable. For offer construction detail, see [offers.md](offers.md). *Example: A $997 course: core training ($1,997 value) + implementation templates ($497 value) + private community ($297/year value) + 1-hour Q&A call ($500 value) = $3,291 total value, offered for $997, 30-day money back guarantee.*

**Templates:**

```
Hook formula:
[Counterintuitive claim or sharp specific result] — without [expected sacrifice or common method].

Epiphany Bridge opening:
"I was [relatable struggling situation] when I discovered [new mechanism]. Here is what happened…"

Offer stack:
[Product]: $[value]. [Bonus 1 that resolves objection]: $[value]. [Bonus 2]: $[value].
Total value: $[sum]. Today: $[actual price]. Plus: [guarantee].
```

**Watch out:**
- Putting the offer before the story — the prospect's sales resistance is high; the story must first transfer belief
- Using a generic hook ("Learn the secrets of marketing") that fails to pattern interrupt because it is identical to every other ad in the feed
- Epiphany Bridge without a genuine wall/desperation moment — the story falls flat without the emotional low point that makes the epiphany meaningful
- Stacking offers with vague bonuses — every bonus must address a named objection; random bonuses add noise, not value
- Skipping the hook entirely and opening with your story — without a hook, the prospect is not yet stopped and listening
