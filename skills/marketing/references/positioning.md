# Positioning — own a place in the mind

Positioning is the deliberate choice of market context — category, competitive alternative, and differentiated value — that sets the buyer's evaluation yardstick before they assess a single feature. Done right, it makes your product's value obvious without explanation. Do positioning before writing copy ([copy.md](copy.md)) or pricing an offer ([offers.md](offers.md)).

---

### April Dunford's Obviously Awesome: 5 Components of Positioning — April Dunford, Obviously Awesome

*Positioning is defined by five interdependent components that, taken together, make your product's value obvious to the right buyer in the right context.*

**When:** When a product exists and has some customers but prospects don't "get it," the sales cycle is long, or the team argues about who the product is for and how to describe it. Run this before writing any messaging, updating a website, or entering a new market.

| Component | What it is | The question to answer | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Competitive Alternatives | What the customer would actually use if your product didn't exist — including spreadsheets, manual processes, do-nothing, and "hire an intern." This is the JTBD baseline: what else could do the job? Status quo wins 20–30% of deals. | "If we didn't exist, what would this customer do?" Pull from lost-deal notes and discovery recordings. List 2–5 real substitutes — not vendors prospects have never heard of. | Userlist found their real alternatives were manual follow-ups, in-house builds, ConvertKit, and Intercom — not "every email automation vendor." |
| 2. Unique Attributes | The specific features, capabilities, or characteristics your product has that the competitive alternatives do not. An attribute only qualifies as a differentiator if it is provably absent from what customers would otherwise use. Every claimed attribute needs proof. | "For each alternative, what does our product do or have that this alternative does not? Can we prove it?" | Janna Systems (CRM) differentiated on modeling relationships between people (not just accounts) and mapping influence networks — capabilities absent from Siebel. |
| 3. Value (and Proof) | The business outcome the customer gets from each unique attribute — the answer to "so what?" Must be backed by evidence: metrics, retention data, ROI figures, speed gains. Strip adjectives ("powerful," "easy") and replace with measurable claims. | "For each attribute, what does this enable for the buyer? What proof supports it?" Cluster related attributes into 3–5 value themes, each with attached evidence. | Userlist's simpler implementation + lower pricing + less complex interface clustered into one theme: "Get started with less effort and resources," proven by time-to-first-campaign benchmarks vs. Intercom. |
| 4. Target Market Characteristics | The specific, observable traits of the buyer segment that make them care intensely about your value. Not demographics — situational characteristics that predict whether a prospect will immediately see the value: their constraints, stack, role, pain intensity, growth stage. | "What do our best customers (fast close, low churn, referrals) have in common that explains why they saw the value immediately?" Find the causal trait, not the demographic proxy. | Userlist's target was bootstrapped or small-team SaaS founders with DIY necessity, selective time allocation, and no budget for enterprise complexity — not "VP of Marketing at B2B SaaS." |
| 5. Market Category | The label that sets the buyer's entire evaluation frame: what features to expect, what to pay, who the other vendors are, what success looks like. Wrong category = evaluated on criteria where you lose. Three strategies: (1) Head-to-head, (2) Big fish, small pond, (3) Create a new category (expensive). | "What existing category, if any, triggers assumptions that make our differentiated value immediately obvious?" Test with target buyers: what do they expect, compare to, and pay when they hear the label? | Janna Systems shifted from "CRM" (Siebel dominated) to "CRM for Investment Banks" (a subsegment where relationship-mapping was the #1 criterion). Revenue: $2M → $80M in 18 months. |

**Templates:**

> `For [target market characteristics], who are currently using [competitive alternatives], our product is a [market category] that provides [value themes]. Unlike [competitive alternatives], we [unique attributes + proof].`

> `Competitive alternatives audit: "If our product didn't exist tomorrow, what would [customer name] do to solve [job to be done]?"`

> `Attribute-to-value bridge: "[Unique attribute] means that [target market] can [business outcome], unlike [alternative] where [limitation]."`

> `Category selection test: "If I tell a [target buyer] our product is a [proposed category], what do they assume about features, price, and competitors? Does that assumption set us up to win or lose?"`

**Watch out:**
- Listing features as value instead of outcomes — "real-time sync" is an attribute; "never lose a deal because data was stale" is the value.
- Defining competitive alternatives as vendors you wish you competed against, not what buyers actually use (the "phantom competitor" trap).
- Picking a market category based on prestige rather than what triggers the right buyer assumptions — calling yourself an "AI platform" when buyers need "compliance automation tool."
- Treating target market as a demographic ("mid-market B2B") instead of a causal characteristic that explains purchase motivation.
- Running this exercise with marketing alone — sales knows real competitive alternatives; customer success knows real value. Without them, output is aspirational fiction.
- Skipping proof on unique attributes — unverified claims collapse in competitive sales and produce messaging internal teams don't believe.
- Using the same positioning for every buyer persona — different buyers care about different value themes; one positioning statement served to all is positioning for none.

### April Dunford's Obviously Awesome: 10-Step Positioning Process — April Dunford, Obviously Awesome

*A sequenced workshop process that takes a cross-functional team from existing customer evidence through documented, shared positioning ready to deploy in sales and marketing.*

**When:** When a company needs to run an actual positioning exercise — not just understand the theory. Requires 2–5 people (marketing, sales, customer success, a business owner), a block of focused time (typically 1–2 days of workshops), and access to best-customer data. Run when launching, repositioning, or when sales and marketing are misaligned on who the product is for.

| Step | What it is | The question to answer | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Understand the customers who love your product | Identify best-fit customers: short sales cycle, low churn, referrals, will take a reference call. They reveal what positioning should be — they already see the value clearly. Everyone else is noise. | "Which customers bought quickly, rarely churn, and refer others? Interview 5–10 of them: why did you buy, what did you consider, what changed after?" | Userlist identified bootstrapped SaaS founders who signed up quickly and referred others — these became the entire positioning archetype. |
| 2. Form a positioning team | Positioning done by marketing alone produces output sales ignores. Must include marketing, sales, customer success, and an executive sponsor with authority to make calls. | "Who in the organization hears real competitive alternatives, knows post-purchase value, and can champion the output?" Brief everyone: this is a strategic decision, not a branding exercise. | VP Sales (hears "how does this compare to X?"), CS lead (knows actual use), CMO (owns messaging), CEO (makes category calls). |
| 3. Align vocabulary and release positioning baggage | Teams carry outdated assumptions from the founding story or early market entry. These operate as invisible constraints. Surface and explicitly release them before analysis begins. | "Have each team member independently write how they'd describe the product. Compare answers. Name the disagreements and outdated framings, then formally set them aside." | Arm & Hammer had to release "baking ingredient company" before seeing that deodorizing capability enabled a completely different category. |
| 4. List your true competitive alternatives | What buyers would actually use if your product didn't exist — not the competitors on your website, but real substitutes in the buyer's decision process, including non-software options. This is the JTBD lens. | "What shows up on shortlists alongside us? In the last 10 lost deals, what did prospects say they'd do instead?" Pull from lost-deal data. Output: 3–6 real alternatives. | Userlist's sales team identified four actual alternatives: manual follow-ups, in-house builds, general email tools (Drip, ConvertKit), and enterprise messaging (Intercom, Customer.io). |
| 5. Isolate your unique attributes and features | Every feature, capability, or characteristic your product has that the Step 4 alternatives do not. Every item must be supported by proof — data, certifications, customer evidence, benchmarks. Opinions don't count. | "For each alternative, what does our product have that it does not? What proof exists for each attribute? Strike anything unproven." | A data pipeline tool lists: sub-5-minute deployment (vs. days for Fivetran), schema-on-read flexibility, no-code transformations — each needing a proof source. |
| 6. Map attributes to value themes | Translate each unique attribute into the customer outcome it enables. Cluster related attributes and outcomes into 3–5 value themes. Move from "what the product does" to "what the customer gains." | "For each attribute: so what for the customer? What business outcome does this enable?" Cluster into ≤5 themes, named in outcome language, not feature language. | Userlist mapped "simpler implementation + lower pricing + less complex interface" into one theme: "Get started with less effort and resources." |
| 7. Determine who cares a lot | Not all buyers value your value themes equally. Identify the specific situational or contextual buyer traits that predict a strong reaction to your value — traits that make someone a best-fit prospect. | "For each value theme, what kind of buyer would find this critical — not nice-to-have, but mission-critical? List observable characteristics." These become ICP criteria. | For Userlist's "less effort" theme: bootstrapped or small-team founders with no dedicated ops resources who needed to ship quickly. A well-funded team with DevOps wouldn't care as much. |
| 8. Find a market frame of reference (and choose how to position in it) | Select the category label that triggers buyer assumptions aligned with your strengths. Three strategies: (1) Head-to-head, (2) Big fish, small pond, (3) Create a new category. Test candidate labels with real buyers. | "If I told you this is a [category], what would you expect it to do, compare it to, and pay?" If answers highlight your strengths — use it. If they highlight your weaknesses — choose differently. | Janna Systems chose "big fish, small pond": "CRM for Investment Banks" made relationship-mapping the category's defining criterion. Revenue: $2M → $80M in 18 months. |
| 9. Layer on a trend (carefully) | Optionally connect positioning to a relevant macro trend — technology shift, regulatory change, market movement — that makes your product timely. Adds "why now?" Warning: the link must be immediately obvious to buyers without explanation. | "Is there a trend that makes our value more urgent right now? Does a target buyer immediately see the connection without explanation?" If they say "I don't see how that's related" — drop it. | A compliance automation tool in 2022 could layer on "the rise of SOC 2 requirements for enterprise sales" — buyers immediately connect the urgency. A project management tool claiming relevance to "AI transformation" without a clear mechanism creates confusion. |
| 10. Capture your positioning so it can be shared | Document the complete positioning in a single internal reference showing all five components and how they connect. This is not an external tagline — it is an internal positioning map. Without documentation, workshop alignment dissolves within weeks. | "Does every function — marketing, sales, product, leadership — have a shared document they can use as the source of truth for messaging, pitches, and roadmap?" Review and update when market conditions shift. | Userlist's document captured: alternatives, differentiators, value, target, category. It aligned their website rewrite, sales scripts, and onboarding copy in one artifact. |

**Templates:**

> `Step 1 customer interview: "Walk me through the moment you decided to buy. What were you using before? What made you look for something new?"`

> `Step 4 sales debrief: "In the last 10 deals we lost, what did the prospect say they would do instead of buying from us?"`

> `Step 5 proof audit: "[Attribute]: Proven by [source/data/customer quote with metric]. Absent from [alternative] because [reason]."`

> `Step 6 value translation: "[Attribute] means our buyers can [outcome], which matters because [pain it removes or goal it enables]."`

> `Step 8 category test script: "If I told you this tool is a [candidate category], what would you expect it to do, what would you compare it to, and what would you expect to pay?"`

**Watch out:**
- Running the exercise with marketing only — sales' knowledge of actual competitive alternatives and real objections is irreplaceable; without it, Step 4 output is wrong and everything downstream is built on fiction.
- Skipping Step 3 (releasing positioning baggage) — teams sprint to Step 4 carrying old positioning as an assumption and simply validate it rather than challenging it.
- Treating Step 8 as a naming exercise rather than a strategic decision — picking a category because it sounds modern ("AI-native platform") rather than testing whether it triggers the right buyer assumptions.
- Doing Step 9 first — leading with a trend before establishing clear differentiation produces buzzword-laden positioning nobody believes.
- Completing the exercise and then not documenting (Step 10) — verbal workshop alignment dissolves in 30 days; without a shared document, each team reverts to their own interpretation.
- Treating the output as permanent — revisit when the competitive landscape shifts, a new dominant alternative emerges, or the best-fit customer profile changes.
- Conflating the positioning document (internal) with the external tagline — the document contains the full strategic logic; external messaging is derived from it, not the same artifact.

## Named tools (use them — but they are not the authority)

Per the skill's evidence-first stance (see [EXPERTS.md](../EXPERTS.md)), these popular frameworks are useful scaffolds but did not survive the credibility audit as authorities:

- **StoryBrand SB7 (Donald Miller)** — a 7-part messaging wireframe: a (1) **Character** (the customer, as hero) who wants something (2) has a **Problem**, (3) meets a **Guide** (your brand) who (4) gives a **Plan**, (5) **Calls** them to action, that helps them (6) avoid **Failure** and (7) ends in **Success**. A solid first-draft scaffold for a homepage or about-page; but it yields formulaic, lookalike messaging — use it to get unstuck, then differentiate. Don't treat Miller as evidence of what actually drives revenue.
- **Ries & Trout positioning vocabulary** — *own a single word or idea in the prospect's mind*; **Law of Duality** (mature markets converge on two dominant players); **Flanking** (attack an uncontested attribute, segment, or price tier the leader ignores). Excellent hypothesis-generators. But the "22 Immutable Laws" framing is marketing, not science (empirically refuted; predicted the iPhone would fail) — test, don't obey.
