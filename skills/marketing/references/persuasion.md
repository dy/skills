# Persuasion — the primitives

These are cross-cutting levers, not standalone tactics — layer them into offers ([offers.md](offers.md)), copy ([copy.md](copy.md)), and distribution ([distribution.md](distribution.md)). They work because they match how people actually decide: evolved social heuristics running beneath conscious reasoning. Misused, they read as manipulation and backfire — often permanently.

---

### Cialdini's 7 Principles of Persuasion — Robert Cialdini, Influence (1984) / Pre-Suasion (2016)

*Seven psychological levers — rooted in evolved social heuristics — that reliably move people toward yes when applied before, during, or after a persuasive ask.*

**When:** Any time you need to increase compliance, conversion, or cooperation: product launches, email sequences, landing pages, sales conversations, onboarding flows, fundraising asks, or policy adoption campaigns. Reach for the specific principle whose mechanism matches the friction blocking the yes.

| Principle | Mechanism (why it works) | Apply it (ethical marketing tactic) | Over-use warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Reciprocity** | People feel obligated to return favors. The norm triggers automatically — even for unsolicited gifts — and scales with perceived generosity, unexpectedness, and personalization. Regan (1971): recipients of an unsolicited Coke bought twice as many raffle tickets from the giver. | Give first, give genuinely, give before the ask. The gift must feel free of strings to activate the norm. Personalize it ("for you specifically"). Make the give meaningful enough that not reciprocating would feel uncomfortable. *Template: "Before we talk about [product], here is [specific free resource] that solves [exact problem] — no strings attached."* | Attaching conditions ("free guide — just enter your email") converts the gift into a transaction and kills the obligation. |
| **2. Commitment & Consistency** | Once people commit — especially publicly, in writing, or through effortful action — they align subsequent behavior to protect a consistent self-image. Mechanism: cognitive dissonance. Commitments are strongest when active, public, effortful, and freely chosen. | Extract a small, genuine yes that aligns with your larger ask. Frame the main offer as consistent with that earlier commitment. Sequence: micro-yes → medium-yes → full offer. *Template: "You told us [small prior commitment]. Here is [next step] that's consistent with that."* | Manufactured micro-commitments (dark patterns, forced clicks) backfire — users reject the larger ask harder to protect their autonomy. |
| **3. Social Proof** | Under uncertainty, people infer correct behavior from what similar others are doing. Effect amplifies with (a) number of others and (b) similarity of those others. Cialdini's towel-reuse study (Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius, 2008, *Journal of Consumer Research*): "Most guests in this room reuse towels" outperformed generic environmental appeals by ~26%. | Display proof from the most similar possible cohort to your target — not the most impressive cohort. Specificity beats scale: "4,312 SaaS founders" outperforms "10,000 businesses." Match proof to funnel stage: volume proof for acquisition, similar-case testimonials for decision. | Showing proof from the wrong cohort (enterprise logos to SMB prospects) signals misfit. Generic star ratings have become visual noise. |
| **4. Authority** | People defer to credible experts on complex or high-stakes decisions. Authority cues: titles, credentials, track record, relevant third-party endorsement. Cialdini's key point: authority must be signaled **before** the ask, not assumed. | Demonstrate credentials in the specific domain of the ask before making it. Third-party validation outperforms self-assertion. Match authority type to audience: practitioners trust track records; academics trust peer citations; consumers trust recognizable institutional logos. | Authority in one domain does not transfer to unrelated domains — celebrity endorsements outside their expertise register as inauthentic and reduce trust. |
| **5. Liking** | People comply more readily with people they like. Three drivers: (a) similarity — shared identity, values, or background; (b) genuine compliments — flattery works even when recipients know it, if it contains a grain of truth; (c) cooperative association — working toward a shared goal. *Distinct from Unity: Liking is individual-to-individual affinity; Unity is shared group identity.* | Uncover real similarities between your brand persona and your audience before pitching. Mirror their language; reference their real struggles. Use specific, genuine compliments rather than generic praise. | Fabricated similarity or fake compliments are detectable and destroy trust permanently. The mechanism requires genuine common ground. |
| **6. Scarcity** | People assign higher value to rare or dwindling opportunities. Mechanism: loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky) — the pain of losing access is felt roughly 2× more intensely than the pleasure of gaining it. Two types: quantity scarcity ("only 3 left") and time scarcity ("offer ends Friday"). | Only communicate scarcity that is real. State the specific constraint *and its reason*: "We cap beta cohorts at 50 so each founder gets a live Q&A slot" is more persuasive than a bare countdown because it explains the why. Frame CTA in loss language: "Claim your spot" over "Sign up." Booking.com's "Only 2 rooms left at this price" works because the scarcity is genuine (dynamic inventory) and specific (this price tier). | Fake scarcity — evergreen countdown timers that reset, permanently "low stock" badges — creates urgency fatigue and, when discovered, destroys brand credibility. Consumers are increasingly scarcity-literate. |
| **7. Unity** *(added Pre-Suasion, 2016)* | People are most influenced by those they perceive as part of the same "we" — same family, tribe, political group, or tight-knit community. Shared identity creates in-group favoritism: we assign positive intent and higher credibility to in-group members. Cialdini notes Unity acts as an accelerator — when present, all other principles work more effectively. *Original six are from Influence (1984); Unity is the seventh, introduced in Pre-Suasion (2016).* | Identify the real group identity your best customers already claim and make it explicit in your positioning. Co-creation rituals strengthen Unity. Use "us/we" language grounded in a label the audience already holds, not an aspirational one you're projecting onto them. Notion's early positioning — "builders and makers who refuse to use broken tools" — activated Unity, not just Liking, producing extreme in-group word-of-mouth. | Claiming a group identity your brand does not authentically belong to reads as pandering. Unity built on performative identity signaling is immediately recognized as manipulation. |

**Watch out:**

- **Reciprocity** — conditions on the gift kill the norm entirely.
- **Commitment** — must be freely chosen; coerced micro-yeses produce harder rejection downstream.
- **Social proof** — wrong cohort signals misfit, not validation.
- **Authority** — domain-matched only; borrowed credibility from mismatched experts reduces trust.
- **Liking** — fabricated similarity is detectable and permanently damaging.
- **Scarcity** — fake scarcity is increasingly detected; when caught, it collapses brand credibility.
- **Unity** — performative identity claims read as manipulation.
- **Over-stacking** — deploying all seven simultaneously signals desperation, not persuasion. Match the principle to the specific friction blocking the yes.

> ⚠️ Specific conversion-uplift percentages and A/B test figures (from CXL, Drip, Booking.com case studies) cited in secondary literature should be treated as illustrative benchmarks, not audited figures. The 26% towel-reuse figure is primary source: Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius (2008), *Journal of Consumer Research*.

## Ethics (non-negotiable)

Persuasion principles amplify trust — or destroy it. Use **real** scarcity, **real** social proof, **real** authority. Manufactured proof or faked independence is the line between a durable brand and an FTC consent order (Joe Sugarman, 1989). The honest application compounds across a career; the dishonest one is a one-time withdrawal against a reputation you don't get back. If a principle only "works" by misleading the prospect, it isn't working — it's borrowing against the future.
