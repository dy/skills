# Kovodstvo — Artemy Lebedev's Design Reference

Dense digest of Kovodstvo ("The Guide"), §§ 0–191 published. Organized by theme, not by section order. § numbers are anchors back to the original — cite them when quoting.

---

## 1. Design philosophy

### Design is solving a problem, not decorating

- **Design isn't up for discussion — it *is* the solution to the stated task.** The only valid question: has the task been solved? (§ 149)
- Design is problem-solving, not "making things beautiful" — beauty can legitimately be *part* of the brief, but it is never the whole task. (§ 149)
- Studio's mission: find the most convenient, simple, and beautiful way to solve the stated task, without losing the meaning along the way. (§ 149)
- Bottlenecks on wine bottles are narrow because cork was historically expensive — not because "the designer's left testicle suddenly itched," a random itch of inspiration. Design outcomes trace to material/cost/functional constraints, not artistic whim. (§ 140)
- Test for a junior designer: invent your own brief and execute it well — real ability shows in self-driven, completed work. (§ 149)

### Simplicity ≠ primitiveness — and the fear of simplicity

- **Design must be simple** («Дизайн должен быть простым») — the entire content of § 108 is this one line; the aphorism's brevity performs its own thesis.
- **Design must be simple, but not primitive** («Дизайн должен быть простым, но не примитивным») (§ 147) — simplicity is intentional restraint that keeps meaningful detail; primitiveness is mere absence of consideration. Facades with cornices read as more humane than bare ones — bareness isn't sophistication, it's emptiness. True simplicity requires knowing *which* details matter enough to keep.
- Epigraph: "Simplicity is the necessary condition of the beautiful" (Tolstoy, cited § 57) — simplicity is a precondition of beauty, not a lesser achievement. Wrong instinct to fear: even a fool can do it simply.
- **Fear of simplicity** (§ 89): designers over-complicate out of insecurity, believing plainness won't command a high fee, so they add ornament to look "finished." Test case: 30 designers asked to lay out elevator buttons for a 23-story building — only one used the obvious vertical arrangement; the rest proposed circular/spiral layouts to look "cool." **"Originality for originality's sake is worthless"** — it "becomes obsolete the day after creation." Hard line: "A designer unable to find simple solutions shouldn't work with clients."
- **Any interface can be simplified by half** («Любой интерфейс можно упростить в два раза») (§ 110) — Case: a "minimal" remote with power, channel, and volume rocker still has redundancy (power and channel are logically identical binary switches). Designers habitually repeat the same information across channels (shape, text, direction, plus/minus symbols duplicating a graphic already shown) — cut it.
- Counterpoint — **complication has its place**: complication, like simplification, is a sophisticated technique and demands judgment (§ 111). Deliberate redundancy is sometimes correct: two connected Metro stations both named "Prospekt Mira" are shown as two circles with a transfer corridor, not one ambiguous merged circle. Roundabout signage layers multiple conventions to remove ambiguity; a Spanish highway posts four warning signs at one deceptive bend for guaranteed visibility from multiple angles. Strategic redundancy across channels can beat elegant minimalism when safety is at stake.

### Logic before aesthetics

- **In interfaces, logic outranks aesthetics** («В интерфейсах логика важнее эстетики») (§ 109). Not license for ugliness, but skepticism toward "painting a corpse." Two workflows exist — solve logic then retrofit aesthetics, or the reverse — Lebedev favors the former. **However you repaint a Russian-made vehicle, it won't drive any better for it.** Every design task is fundamentally about building the "interface — the contact point between person and object." Model: Harry Beck's 1933 London Underground map, logic-first design that became the global metro-map standard.

### "In design there are no laws"

- **In design there are no laws. There never were, and never will be** («В дизайне законов нет. Не было и не будет.») (§ 99) — there are no absolute design laws, only situational guidelines. Systematically knocks down "rules" (one typeface only, limited palettes, fixed logo placement, mandatory minimalism, "300 DPI always," fixed reading-time) by showing legitimate exceptions to each.
- Treat every design rule — including every rule in this very digest — as context-dependent guidance, not universal law. Contrast: alcohol advertising and telecom advertising can use opposite approaches and both work. Designers and clients suffer when a situational guideline gets enforced as a universal constraint.
- **Principles vs. rules** (§ 176): you can do almost anything creatively if it's grounded in a clear principle. Example principles: always discard the first sketch shown; write all headlines without adjectives; end all product names in "-us"; paint the side panel orange. **Having *a* principle is one of the most convenient forms of constraint** — its content matters less than its presence. Work without any principle at all is useless and meaningless.
- **The rule for making rules** (§ 178): make something good *first*, then find the patterns in it — reversing the order produces a Procrustean bed that mangles content to fit a premature rule. **First make it good, then look for the patterns.** Modular grids exemplify the failure: math imposed on a blank page forces content into awkward cells instead of arranging content until harmonious and extracting the grid after. **Good rules are rooted in soul** — not top-down formalism.
- **In design, everything that's forbidden is permitted** (§ 187) — deliberately provocative, thin on elaboration; design as a domain that transcends ordinary constraint.

### Method of progressive JPEG

- **At any second, a project is 100% done — even though its execution depth might be at only 4%** (§ 167), modeled on progressive JPEG rendering. Depth of execution scales with time available — sketch to pixel-perfect, both legitimately "complete" because the core idea is already whole.
- **I can solve almost any design problem in the time it takes to state it.** **Time buys only the degree of doneness — the steak's shape is clear from the start.**

### Unit of meaning

- Measure designer output in "units of meaning" (intellectually caloric ideas), not hours or file count (§ 148). A 100-page brand book = one unit; a logo counts, slapping it on a business card doesn't.
- Benchmarks: average good designer ≈ 1–2 units/month; excellent designer ≈ 4/month; an art director needs ≥6/month to justify overseeing three designers.
- Self-test: write down actual ideas produced over 3 months — most people find fewer than 3, often stale. Honest tracking exposes disengagement.

### Design by reversal

- Imagine the worst possible solution in full detail, then flip it (§ 150). Shop entrance: hide the door, put it on the 3rd floor, remove the handle, make it jam. Website: cryptic URLs, excess graphics, fragmented content, indistinguishable links.
- Critical, easy-to-skip final step: **the main thing is not to forget to flip everything back again.** The reversal *is* the design; skipping it just leaves the bad version.

### The value of constraints

- A designer given zero constraints is paralyzed, not liberated (§ 175). **There's no creativity without constraints. The best design examples have come from the harshest requirements.**
- Complaining about client constraints (color, logo size) signals unprofessionalism. **Good design is born only within constraints.**
- **Creative crisis** (§ 162): word-listing exercise — asked to recite known words, people run dry after ~20; asked to narrate something, no shortage appears. The gap is execution, not raw material. **A crisis of ideas can only arise when a designer sets out to invent something original and unusual for its own sake.** **The original and unusual can't be invented on purpose — it can only emerge on its own, in the process of working.** **Creative crisis is a dead end on a meaningless path.**

### Pyramid, resolution, and other diagnostic frames

- **Lebedev's pyramid** (§ 153): in any company, organization, or gathering the same ratio holds — a small competent minority at top, a much larger low-effort majority beneath. Real contributors are always a thin top layer. (Page withholds diagram text; reconstructed from secondary sources.)
- **Low resolution** (§ 132): "resolution" = ability to distinguish styles, objects, colors, fonts, voices — determines professional competence. **A designer's resolution usually approaches one** — barely any discrimination at all. **A designer is one of the most ambitious, uneducated, superficial professionals there is** — rivaled only by journalists. Resolution rises only through experience, desire, and luck. Symptom: designers who celebrate cheap effects, over-decorate with headers, fear unfamiliar choices.
- **Design is war** («Дизайн — это война») (§ 156): graphic design is a weapon in an ongoing conflict of interest — case study: cigarette health warnings vs. tobacco manufacturers, an escalating arms race with no end. **Design is war.** Each side hides its next move and studies the opponent's response.
- **Creative tools** (§ 181): **a designer's tool is Photoshop; an art director's tool is the designer.** People, not software, are the instrument one level up.
- **On creative development** (§ 186): **those raised on building blocks grow up to invent transformers; those raised on transformers grow up to invent building blocks.** Creative development is compensatory, not continuous — creators build what was missing from their own childhood.
- **Inoculation against reality** (§ 19): bad design persists culturally, not materially — self-administer an inoculation against your surroundings; refuse to normalize the mediocrity around you (ninety-eight percent… made by dilettantes). **Only those who got the inoculation will survive.**
- **Young and seasoned** (§ 20): **the ability to judge good design is no smaller an achievement than the ability to create it** — don't trust untrained judgment, including your own early on. Watch for false praise — clients approve work for the wrong reasons while missing the actual purpose it was all created for.
- **The murdered generation** (§ 36): **the environment itself is to blame** — visual taste is shaped by environment, not personal failing (he blames Khrushchev-era mass housing standardization, the "khrushchoby"). Bad taste isn't personal incompetence; actively seek exposure to better work to recalibrate your own eye.
- **Variety** (§ 185): zoo lemurs eat only bananas but stay content because keepers cut the fruit differently every day. **Even with total monotony of resources, the one key to success is variety.** Formal variation sustains engagement even when underlying substance never changes.
- **A shelf in the brain** (§ 190): **the human brain is comparable to a huge card catalog** — neurons are lazy, comfortable with the habitual. **What separates a good designer from an ordinary one is the ability to build new neural connections in people's brains** — a whole new cabinet of drawers, not just filling an existing slot. Pushback against new design is itself a sign the restructuring is working.
- **Art** (§ 191): **art must be either disposable or utilitarian** — either one-time (dance, food, music, massage — pleasure in the moment, then only memory) or utilitarian (paintings, architecture, logos, signage — reusable, revisitable). Everything else is a consciousness error — museum-preserved one-time-art-as-relic is nonsensical.

### Format vs. cloning

- Distinguish cloning (copying another's structure/imagery wholesale) from *format* — a set of shared rules/traits across a whole category (newsprint's paper/columns/headline sizes) (§ 25). Resembling the category leader isn't theft if you're fulfilling the format's own requirements. A format is born when someone solves a problem so well that followers must converge on it with only minor variation (e.g., web directories echoing Yahoo's two-column link layout).
- **Don't be afraid to reuse someone else's schema — the real skill is inventing something new within it, every time.**

### Encoding meaning through form (§ 106, § 107)

- Encoding = **giving an object or entity additional, distinguishable properties**, so it communicates beyond bare function.
- Channels, in order of primacy: symbolic (icons, numerals), formal (shape itself carries information — salt vs. pepper shakers differ by hole count; chess pieces' distinct forms determine legal moves), positional (location in space — reliable for only ~6 positions in 3D, ~9 on a 2D plane), then color/pattern/sound once those are exhausted.
- Functional encoding via form is safety-critical: identical Soviet outlets across different voltages (220V/127V) destroyed equipment; identical radio plugs caused fires. **Different functions must get different physical forms.**
- Design so meaning survives when the primary code fails — colorblindness affects 8% of men; the 1908 London Underground map shows color's power, but modern TfL maps still work in monochrome via redundant pattern coding.

### Anticipating misreading (§ 92)

- **A designer must avoid anything that could be misread** — especially for high-stakes industries (airlines, nuclear, transport).
- Worked failures: a Swiss air-traffic-control logo depicting a descending plane, grimly literal after a real mid-air collision days later; an airline site defaulting the destination field to "doesn't matter"; an airline ad's falling feather after a plane vanishes into clouds, unintentionally evoking a bird strike.
- For safety-sensitive brands, actively pre-search imagery/copy for worst-case literal or morbid readings before shipping.

### Cultural contexts (§ 73)

- Meaning is culturally relative, not universal — colors, symbols, even a rainbow's "seven colors" are convention, not fact. Every group runs on its own context marking "our people"; a designer must consciously identify the audience's context, not assume a universal one.
- **A designer's power lies in human weakness** — design's power lies in human self-deception, but that leverage usually only works inside a familiar cultural frame; exported unchanged, it fails or embarrasses.
- Don't design for a foreign market using only your own cultural context — local blind spots are invisible from outside, hence the value (often undervalued) of genuinely local designers.

### Cultivating taste, not indulging it (§ 34, § 35)

- Design/advertising must either match existing audience taste to sell, or better, reshape taste over time through sustained, low-key cultural programs (his analogy: iodized salt, Moscow Metro architecture) — **it takes cultivation**, not a one-off campaign.
- Staff realistically for both tiers a real project needs: unglamorous high-volume production labor and the rarer creative/strategic work — the market underprices the former and oversupplies people chasing the image of the latter.

## 2. Russian typography & punctuation — apply when the reviewed artifact is in Russian

### Dash, minus, and hyphen — three distinct marks (§ 97, § 62)

Universal principle: the hyphen, the minus sign, and the dash are three different characters with three different jobs, not interchangeable substitutes for whatever key happens to be on the keyboard.

- **Hyphen (-)**: joins particles (кто-либо, где-то), prefixes (во-первых, по-русски), abbreviations, compound words (ковёр-самолёт), line-break hyphenation. Aligns optically with lowercase letters.
- **Minus (−)**: math/formulas only; aligns with cap-height/numerals; often missing from ordinary fonts — don't substitute a hyphen.
- **Dash (—)**: replaces omitted sentence elements, separates subject/predicate, marks direct speech, indicates a pause, denotes a route/range (Москва — Санкт-Петербург). Width ≈ "M"; always spaced both sides. **Don't confuse the dash with the hyphen — it's normally two to three times longer.**
- Root cause of confusion: most designers use only the keyboard-accessible hyphen for all three roles, collapsing distinctions that exist for real typographic reasons.
- **The short dash (§ 158)**: Russian typesetting has adopted the en dash for numeric ranges (2000–2009), following Anglo-American convention — the classical long dash looks oversized in that role today. The long dash keeps its normal textual role (Серп и Молот — Карачарово); phone numbers keep hyphens/spaces regardless.

### Quotation marks (§ 104)

Universal principle: quotation marks are paired, directional, and language-specific — never generic straight "programmer's quotes." Straight quotes were a typewriter cost-saving hack, later inherited uncritically by computers once technical editors stopped supervising layout. They're acceptable in code/technical contexts only.

Russian specifics: outer « » (guillemets), inner „ " . Check pre-computer print editions (USSR before 1990; West before 1985) for the correct marks per language. **A self-respecting designer** always uses them correctly.

### Ellipsis (§ 164)

**The ellipsis shouldn't be a byte-saving shortcut character.** In books and refined typesetting, only three separate periods (...) are correct — not the single Unicode ellipsis glyph (…).

### The double comma (§ 133)

Speculative proposal (not an established rule): a new mark, «двоезапятие» (literally "double comma," two commas set together), to cover the gap left by underused semicolons in Russian writing — unlike foreign writers, Russians largely avoid the semicolon. Framed as filling a genuine functional/intonational gap; acknowledges most typographic inventions never catch on (cites the forgotten 1962 interrobang).

### Hanging punctuation (§ 120)

Universal principle — a hallmark of professional typesetting: optically push punctuation past the text block's edge to compensate for its lighter visual weight versus letters.

- Left: quotation marks, parentheses, bullets hang left of the margin; direct-speech dashes do NOT hang.
- Right: quotation marks, parentheses, periods, commas hang right; hyphens protrude only 1/3–1/2 their length.
- Worthwhile for web text; in print use for newspaper/magazine headlines and large blocks; in books apply universally.

### The letter ё (§ 119)

**Ё should be used**: where it prevents genuine ambiguity; in dictionaries; in books for people learning Russian (children and foreigners); to correctly read rare place names, titles, or surnames. Everywhere else, ё only hurts readability. Most homographs (замок/замо́к — "castle" vs. "lock") survive fine without it. Parallel: English "coöperation" — literate readers don't need the mark.

### Historical and monumental accuracy (§ 23, § 26, § 63, § 139)

- Faking pre-revolutionary orthography by tacking a hard sign (ъ) onto every word ending is a marker of ignorance — the old rules (ять vs. ер, і vs. и, фита vs. ф) were far more complex than a formula. **The best advice: don't attempt period spelling at all** unless you actually know the rules.
- If archaic orthography is used at all, apply it consistently throughout the piece, not just the title — and match typeface to period (a 1901 German modernist face used for an 1885 Imperial-Russian setting is an anachronism a reviewer should flag).
- Case study: the 1992 Cyril-and-Methodius monument in Moscow shipped with five orthographic errors in its Church Slavonic inscription — the letterer chose archaic letterforms for effect without knowing the actual rules; even later "corrections" used mismatched letterforms. **Errors on book covers, in illustrations, on signage — and especially on monuments — are categorically unacceptable**; body text gets more slack, monuments get none.
- Same standard for any published work: **a typo on the title page costs you the job.** Case: a Russian Scrabble edition marketed on "every word counts" misspelled «кроссворд» ("crossword") as "crossoword" on its own packaging.

### Never mix languages (§ 82)

Universal rule: never mix languages within one semantic block — one language per unit of meaning. Corollary: **one language per booklet** — not a multilingual wall of text; for interfaces, use an explicit language switcher rather than co-displaying translations. Cited absurdity: a GE light-bulb box listing "frosted" in 19 languages on one panel.

### Pangrams — proof every glyph (§ 33)

Universal rule: to judge a typeface fairly you must see every glyph it contains — flaws aren't visible from a few sample words. Always proof a new font by setting a full pangram, not cherry-picked text.

Russian note: many Cyrillic fonts hide "monstrous letterforms" in Ф, Д, Л — invisible until you set a pangram that actually uses them.

### The typographic point — not one universal unit (§ 81)

"Point" is not a single universal unit: Anglo-American (PostScript/DTP, exactly 1/72 inch, fixed in the 1980s) and European/Russian (Didot, ≈0.376mm, traced through Fournier 1737 → Didot 1783 → Berthold's metric fix in 1879) are different sizes. Know which system a spec quotes before trusting a point value.

### The keyboard and the comma — small decisions calcify (§ 105)

Soviet ЙЦУКЕН typewriter layouts placed punctuation efficiently after a century of ergonomic refinement. When Western computers arrived, someone "Russified" a Latin keyboard without understanding this tradition and put the comma — used far more often than the period — in the upper register (Shift-required). **Ten minutes of careless work by a random outsider was enough for an awkward, typing-speed-killing layout to get copied everywhere since** — a caution about the outsized, lasting weight of small design decisions.

### Informal typography (§ 135)

Formal typography (newspapers, books) and informal (personal/online communication) split, but informal devices can enrich traditional media too. Strikethrough, originally an ad convention (crossing out old prices), migrated into blogs to imply subtext — works in headlines and body text alike.

**Emoticons are unfairly considered a second-rate device.** Punctuation-based emoticons (`:-)`) are legitimate typography, as historically valid as Roman numerals; graphic image emoticons signal poor taste. Neither device substitutes for actual wit — both demand restraint.

### Spelling "интернет" (§ 55)

Write "интернет" lowercase and decline it normally through all six Russian grammatical cases — don't capitalize, quote, or leave it undeclined or in the Latin alphabet. Don't defer to an "authoritative" source uncritically: Lebedev's request to the Russian Academy of Sciences' language institute came back with its own grammatical errors.

### The ruble, dollar, and euro signs

- **Ruble** (§ 116, superseded by § 159): originally argued the abbreviation "руб."/"р." is the best "symbol" — no glyph needed, works in any font, readable to foreigners without training. **The ruble mark should always follow the number with a space.** **Lebedev later reversed this** (§ 159, 2007): after a law mandated an official symbol, a studio coalition (his included) designed one deliberately rather than leaving it to chance. **"The new symbol has no author; copying and modifying it carries no restrictions."** Adoption of a new symbol depends on bold designers and bold clients reaching critical mass — not decree.
- **Currency placement, universal** (§ 74): symbol follows the amount with a space — "100 $", not "$100" — matching Russian/French/German convention against a US-style prefix.
- **Euro** (§ 77): a case study in bad symbol governance — the € sign (designed by Arthur Eisenmenger, never publicly credited) was released as a rigid, frozen "logo" with mandated construction geometry instead of an adaptable typographic character to be redrawn per typeface. The mandated "always before the number" placement ignored existing local convention (France, Germany, Russia place currency after the amount). Called "one of the ugliest signs," aggravated by a secretive selection process.

### Ampersand (§ 112)

The & is a graphic ligature of Latin *et*, invented by Cicero's secretary Tiro (Notæ Tironianæ, c. 63 BCE) — its history and grammar are universal. In Russian branding, though, it's unnecessary: **the conjunction "и" is already short**, in both sound and shape. Using & in Russian branding manufactures false foreignness/"business" polish — poor taste, like tacking on a hard sign for fake archaism.

### Letter-spacing (§ 79, § 142)

Russian-specific convention: **capitals are always tracked out; lowercase never.** For emphasis in running text, only semi-bold and italic remain legitimate. Forbidden for body-text emphasis: letter-spacing, underlining, color highlighting (typewriter-era holdovers). Bold = headlines/immediate attention; italic = subtler in-text emphasis, literary quotation. Bold-inside-bold → bold italic; italic-inside-italic → upright regular as contrast. Never set whole passages in italic.

### Declension of place names

- **On "-o" endings** (§ 146): toponyms ending in "-o" (Домодедово, Шереметьево) decline like normal Russian nouns, not frozen in the nominative — «После Домодедова еда в Шереметьеве показалась издевательством» ("After Domodedovo, the food at Sheremetyevo felt like mockery").
- **On contested toponyms** (§ 121, polemical/political): prescribes conservative spellings — Таллин (not Таллинн), Алма-Ата (not Алматы), Белоруссия (not Беларусь — "that's a tractor brand"), «поехать на Украину» (not «в Украину») — favors established Russian tradition over post-Soviet renaming.
- **On brand names** (§ 114): "от" is misused to dodge declining brand names («костюм от Армани» instead of the grammatically correct «костюм Армани») — "corporate cowardice" about inflecting trademarks like real Russian words.

### "Вы" written lowercase (§ 165) — three rules

1. **Lowercase "вы"**: never capitalize it — lowercase already conveys respect. «Всё для Вас» ("Everything for You," capitalized) reads as foolish, not respectful — an unbearably obsequious, ad-like manner. Reserve capital "Вы" for maybe three people in a lifetime, genuinely extraordinary address.
2. **Use "свой"**: Russian's reflexive possessive has no English equivalent; don't default to "вашу/вашей" ("your") out of translation habit. «Маша вынула руки из карманов» ("Masha took her hands out of her pockets") needs no possessive at all.
3. **Drop unnecessary "вы"**: avoid the Anglicized «здесь вы можете зарегистрироваться» ("here you can register") — prefer «здесь можно зарегистрироваться» ("registration is available here") or just «Регистрация» ("Registration"). Cutting surplus pronouns strengthens interface text and prose generally.

### Phone numbers, addresses, dates

- **Phone numbers** (§ 91): local "123-45-67"; with city code "(123) 123-45-67"; mobile "+7 123 123-45-67." Group digits in pairs from the right for readability and error-reduction.
- **Postal addresses** (§ 163): reverse the traditional Russian order (large→small) to the international standard (small→large): recipient → company → street/building/apartment → settlement → district → region → postal code → country. The rationale is the postal worker's decoding speed at scale, not the recipient's convenience.
- **Dates** (§ 74): DD.MM.YYYY with periods, not slashes. Lowercase weekdays/months; avoid over-capitalized headlines. Time uses a colon — "15:45," not "15.45." Thousands use a (half-)space separator for numbers of five digits or more. Space between initials: «В. И. Пупкин», never «В.И.Пупкин».

## 3. Screen typography and layout

### Resolution has no inherent meaning

- **Image resolution** (§ 69): a digital image file has no inherent "resolution" — only devices (screen, printer) resolve dots per inch. **Photoshop doesn't care in the least how many dots per inch you assign to an image.** The 72 DPI convention traces to early Macintosh's screen-to-print WYSIWYG mapping. DPI metadata is ignored on-screen by browsers/monitors — it only matters for print sizing. **"Pixel size is like the size of an angel: nobody has exact data."**
- **Screen resolution** (§ 70): screens/GPUs don't actually know their own DPI. Apple picked 72 DPI (true WYSIWYG at the time); Microsoft picked 96 DPI (~1.3×), assuming 1024×768 should fit comfortably on a 14" display. Identical point-sized fonts render physically larger on Windows than on Mac — a 12pt font is 72px tall on a Mac vs. 96px on a PC.

### Pixel graphics and its limits

- **The death of pixel graphics** (§ 71): screen resolution behaves like map scale — more pixels means more detail shown smaller, not "more size." Setting type size in pixels is technically easy but bad practice — prefer resolution-independent (vector) units. As pixel density rises, individual pixels become imperceptible, so purely pixel-based UI chrome (icons, cursors, pixel fonts) is a dead end.
- **Printing pictograms and screenshots** (§ 155): the "everything needs 300 DPI" dogma is wrongly applied to pictograms/screenshots — inherently low-res graphics. **To print a pictogram or screenshot on paper, it's enough to scale it mechanically, with no smoothing.** Interpolation is right for photos, wrong here — it blurs sharp pixel edges. The rough/angular enlarged look is correct, not a defect.
- **The limit of font scaling** (§ 134): metal type physically prevented distortion; DTP unleashed countless examples of unimaginable ugliness. **If a typeface is good, don't touch its design — the result is guaranteed to be worse.** Tolerance ≈ 1–2% compression, imperceptible. Training method: distort your own photograph alongside the font; stop scaling type once facial distortion becomes visible in the photo.
- **GIF compression** (§ 8): GIF compresses row-by-row horizontally — a run of identical horizontal pixels compresses far better than the same run vertically; a 90°-rotated version of the same image can be 3–4× smaller. GIF fits flat graphics (text, lines, solid fills), not photographs.

### Theory of proximity, shared air, symmetry

- **Theory of proximity** (§ 136): **objects placed close together are perceived as related.** **Close together, because connected.** Rooted in Gestalt psychology. A header must sit closer to its own paragraph than to neighboring text — equal spacing above/below creates ambiguity. Illustration: a woman standing equidistant between two men reads as unaffiliated with either. Inverse principle: **objects sharing identical traits are hard to tell apart** — differentiate distinct elements via position, size, weight, or color.
- **Shared air** (§ 166): intrusive web ads are like the childhood prank of waving a hand in someone's face and saying "we're sharing the same air" — not technically a boundary violation, but it feels like one. **Advertising must have its own, clearly bounded territory**, with no right to intrude beyond it without explicit user action. Only a deliberate click specifically to view the ad counts as consent, and even that trigger can't sit inside the main content's space.
- **Symmetry belongs in the trash** («Симметрию — на помойку») (§ 85): **symmetry actively enables designers to wreck meaning** — never apply it for tidiness alone. Failure mode: reordering words purely to visually balance a sign ("Cinema Polyarny Theater" for a theater actually named "Polyarny") sacrifices correct reading order for symmetric shape — measured cost: real readers took 7–30 seconds to parse aloud, often misreading entirely. Symmetry must serve communication; if it breaks reading order or clarity, drop it.
- **Punctuation neutralization** (§ 143): text often looks unruly only because the designer skips neutralization techniques. Tracking: punctuation spacing stays tighter than letter spacing. Centering: center as if no punctuation existed, then place marks after — ellipses especially distort centering if measured in. Match punctuation weight/style to adjacent text (bold punctuation with bold words). Hyperlinks go before quotation marks to avoid the underline clashing with the marks. Footnote numbers go before punctuation, not after. Never mix Russian/English/French typographic conventions in one text. **Good typesetting keeps its hard problems invisible.**

### Color models (§ 40)

Color models are chosen by application, not inherent superiority: **CMYK for print, RGB for screen, LAB for describing and storing any color** — pick the model that fits the medium, not a personal favorite. CMY in theory yields black but in practice yields muddy brown, hence the added K plate — and "K" stands for "key color," not necessarily black, a common misconception to correct. You don't need to invent a new color model — a numeric pattern within an existing space is enough to produce a perceptibly harmonious palette even from a limited set of hues.

### Perspective and vertical lines (§ 101)

The human eye reads all vertical lines as vertical and parallel — any rendering that lets verticals lean or converge incorrectly reads as visually "wrong," regardless of style era. Check architectural/product renders and photography for accidental converging/leaning verticals — most rendering-software defaults get this wrong, and it's immediately perceptible even to non-experts.

**A good illustration is always an additional argument for the product's merits. A bad illustration states an unfortunate truth.**

### The emotion matrix — spatial direction reads as emotion (§ 137)

The sum of "up" and "forward" vectors points to the upper-right corner — **the most "positive" angle in a composition.** Combining vertical (up/down) and directional (forward/back) axes yields 8 emotional zones: top-right maximally positive, bottom-left maximally negative, center calm/balanced.

Case studies: Yukos's descending diagonals plus a downward-pointing triangle read as collapse despite bold type; BP's symmetrical rays toward lowercase letters combine dynamism with stability, reading as positive.

Caveat: applies only to *dynamic* compositional elements, not static objects, and not to multiple compositions sharing one surface — don't over-apply it literally.

### Digital and analog (§ 118)

**The digital discretizes consciousness and breeds mechanicalness; the analog is smooth, though less functional.** Digital wins on precision but risks dehumanizing perception; analog preserves continuity at the cost of capability — weigh this when balancing technical function against human-centered experience.

## 4. Web conventions

### Hypertext fundamentals

- History (§ 54): hypertext predates computing — Gilbert of Poitiers's 12th-century Psalter cross-referencing, Bush's "Memex" (1945), Nelson's "hypertext"/"Xanadu" (1965), Engelbart's first working system (1968), Atkinson's HyperCard. **Since the Renaissance, nothing much has changed except the speed of following links** — a good hyperlink apparatus takes the same care it always did.
- **The secrets of good hypertext** (§ 83): text must survive being detached from its links (e.g., printed out) and still make sense — **if the text is unintelligible once removed from the browser, the hypertext is bad.** Never link from vague words like "here"/"there" — link text itself must carry meaning. Don't over-link: **if every other word is underlined, the reader stops perceiving the text at all.** Prefer clustering links at the end of a passage over mid-sentence scattering. Only link where it adds genuine value. Cross-reference prior related material for full context. Keep link text short (1–2 words), never used purely as a highlighting device.

### Link styling rules (§ 171)

- Links always underlined, even if ugly; never underline non-link text. Don't remove the underline on hover.
- Active links: blue (or black/dark gray); visited: purple. **Links in other colors reliably confuse the reader** — reserve link colors exclusively for links.
- On hover: highlight red/orange or change background/border.
- Dotted underline = in-place toggle/tooltip/calculator; solid underline = actual page navigation.
- Ultimate test overriding all the above: **a viewer should be able to spot a link without touching the mouse.**
- Liberal linking (§ 42): **link to any page on the internet, freely — asking permission first is never necessary** and only stifles the web's basic value mechanism. Warning signs: zero outbound links, a token "Links" page pointing at Microsoft, or 88×31 button link-farms ("one of the most idiotic formats there is").
- No self-links (§ 75): **nothing should link to itself**, because a link implies going somewhere. The logo-as-home-link must be inactive on the homepage itself.

### New windows and forced navigation

- **Opening new windows** (§ 43): **opening windows the user didn't request is a cardinal sin** — the browser window is the user's control surface, not the site's. Only legitimate case: linking to unverified external content, and even then the site must say explicitly it's opening a new window. Unsolicited window-opening is spam.
- **Porn-industry anti-patterns** (§ 41), reject in any review: max two 468×60 banners per page; max six 88×31 buttons (use a real "Links" text section instead); never force links into new windows; never spawn a replacement window when the user closes one; no unsolicited pop-ups (trigger only via explicit action); never hide browser chrome or force fullscreen to block the back button. **Design must be effective without resorting to dishonest tricks.**

### URLs

- **Fighting for clean URLs** (§ 48): plan directory/file names deliberately; keep URLs short and self-explanatory. Prefer English for paths; Russian only for transliteration/already-naturalized loanwords — never mix languages within one path. **A visitor who truncates the address down to the nearest slash should always land on meaningful, predictable content.**
- **Cyrillic domains** (§ 68): don't register/use them — mixing alphabets breaks navigation, Cyrillic percent-encodes into unreadable strings, mail can fail to deliver, the transliterated fallback may be taken, look-alike glyphs enable spoofing (certain Cyrillic letters are visually identical to Latin ones, enough to forge a fake "IBM"), and it requires a compatible keyboard/encoding. Analogy: license plates are Latin-only for global legibility.
- **Duplicate navigation / breadcrumbs** (§ 49): exist to substitute for a URL's own legibility. **Use breadcrumbs wherever content nests logically and there's no sidebar menu.**

### Splash screens (§ 46, § 47)

Splash screens solved a real problem once (pre-auto-detect Cyrillic encoding selection); once that problem vanished, most became pure friction. **If you're not 100% certain a splash screen is necessary, don't make one.** Never use a homepage screenshot as the splash — it produces jarring double-navigation. News/timely sites lose credibility with a splash delay; splash pages belong only to rarely changing content.

If used at all, its core job is to be an entry point — always provide a clear text link in. Flash splashes: always include "skip," never replay on repeat visits, auto-detect rather than ask. **Minimize riddles for new guests** — don't gate access behind timers, unclear icons, or unfinished animations.

### The status bar (§ 50)

The status bar tells the user where a link leads and shows load progress — hiding/overwriting it removes expected information, like taping over a car's speedometer. **You can't tamper with a familiar, standard interface, and you can't hide its elements in situations where people expect them.**

### Sound on websites (§ 51)

The web is a silent, book-like medium by default; users are unprepared for sound. **Never use sound or music decoratively on a website.** Acceptable only when explicitly opted in and carrying real semantic meaning.

### Mobile versions — the position, and its reversal

- **§ 177 (original)**: **the biggest folly in the world is building separate mobile versions of websites.** **Building a mobile version only makes sense with money from clients who have nowhere else to spend it.** Modern smartphones render normal sites correctly; "mobile version" is largely agency sales rhetoric.
- **§ 182 (two years later, partial reversal)**: mobile sites are no longer automatically the worst option, since mobile devices now outnumber computers. **The bigger the screen, the better the interface** (an enduring rule he still holds). **Quality takes a back seat** at this stage of adoption, but **a renaissance always follows a dark age** — treat the current mobile-quality compromise as temporary.

### The main rule of screen interfaces

**No control element should be smaller in area than a 16×16 px square** (§ 168) — baseline for clickable-target usability.

### The two main principles of interface design (§ 76)

1. **Unity of form and content across all control surfaces for one function.** The same function (e.g. volume) must use the same physical control mechanism everywhere, or users never build motor memory: **it's not just inconvenient — it creates real psychological friction, because the user never develops motor memory.** **If we adjust sound with a wheel, sound must be adjusted with a wheel everywhere.**
2. **Two-way communication/feedback.** Any remote control of state must feed the state back to all control surfaces in sync (Radiotehnika R-100 example: adjusting volume remotely must update the remote's own display too).

### Interface engineering needs domain experts, not just software polish (§ 90)

BMW's original iDrive controller: digitizing controls just to look advanced backfired — underused 8-way pad, slow menu navigation for safety-critical functions (traction control buried in menus instead of a dedicated button), physically misplaced controls (CD-eject hit by a passenger's knee). Root cause: disconnect between software engineers and domain (automotive) engineers — a caution against centralizing physical controls into a menu system without domain-aware validation. Charging a premium for a "luxury" interface that is actually worse usability is unnecessary complexity marketed as sophistication — the opposite of quality.

### Pictograms and ideograms (§ 78)

Pictogram = literal depiction (a glass = a glass); ideogram = symbol standing for a broader concept (a glass = drinking culture, statistics) — ideograms are inherently ambiguous without context. Even "self-evident" pictograms often need text to disambiguate.

**A person can instantly see and recognize only a limited number of objects.** Cram too many into one field (dense road-sign clusters) and none of them read. When a pictogram's meaning isn't instantly unambiguous, duplicate it with text.

### Photo captions (§ 169)

**Don't turn a photo credit into an advertisement** — no frames, decorative fonts, prominent placement. **A caption should be visible only when we consciously go looking for it** — low-opacity, discreet, present only if needed.

### Other web/interface conventions

- **"K"/kilo- ambiguity** (§ 84): binary 1024 (memory) vs. decimal 1000 (drives, bandwidth) — always check which base a spec means before quoting a number. Flag as a marketing trick: manufacturers exploit the gap to advertise larger-sounding capacities.
- **404 pages** (§ 93): state the error immediately, including in the tab title; no ads, no long copy, no decorative graphics (logo excepted); don't reuse normal site chrome; explain plainly what happened; preserve the erroneous URL in the address bar.
- **Data preservation** (§ 188): **all data a user has entered is sacred. Always, in every situation.** After a crash/restart/closed window, the user should reopen and continue exactly as if nothing happened — full state restoration, no re-entry. An ethical baseline, not a UX nicety.
- **Skinning/customization** (§ 65): let users customize *how* content looks, never *what* content appears — editorial identity is non-negotiable: **the single most important thing for a publication is to keep its own face and its own voice.** Editorial/news sites forbid customization; service sites (search, mail) may allow it.
- **News on non-news sites** (§ 129): **a homepage news feed is the cheapest way to fake vitality.** If updates come less than monthly, drop the word "news." **Dates next to headlines are pure clutter** — readers learn something's news by reading it. Archive stale items immediately.
- **Informational vs. presentational sites** (§ 56): informational design (a well-marked highway) vs. presentational/eclectic design (an ornamental garden maze) — legitimate but opposite purposes. **A site with no images or shadows is still a real site** — staying usable at any window width is harder and more valuable than a fixed-width graphic showcase. Anything over 5–10 seconds to load loses to faster alternatives.
- **Placeholder content** (§ 66, § 67): placeholder should look like real content, not an obvious stand-in — repeated boilerplate ("text will go here...") is one of the worst effects in a design review. Write custom, plausible placeholder copy instead. Check Lorem Ipsum for accidentally inappropriate corrupted content before shipping.

## 5. Text and language

### Say it shorter (§ 141)

**A message must always account for the conditions under which it's received.** Case: a roadside sign near Taganrog reminding drivers to buckle up fails via ALL CAPS (slows reading), uneven spacing, cramped line spacing, a superfluous direct address ("Driver!"), bureaucratic compound nouns. Fix: direct, imperative, minimal — "Buckle up! Turn on your headlights!" Brevity here isn't a style preference but a functional requirement given speed and limited attention.

### Corporate Russian (§ 114)

**Companies should be ashamed that they find money to renovate the office before they hire an in-house editor.** Corporate marketers mechanically transliterate English into Russian, producing unnatural phrasing (a "Pepsi Generation"-style slogan back-formed word-for-word into stilted Russian instead of translated naturally). Slogans increasingly function as pure graphic elements, unfit for speech: **synthetic copy isn't meant to be spoken aloud.** New logo trends place taglines below the mark in unnatural word order, violating Russian syntax.

### Clichés (§ 123)

"Cliché" and "stereotype" began as pure typographic terms (a printing plate; a cast plate from a matrix) before drifting into general synonyms for banality across Europe. Illustrates how technical vocabulary migrates into metaphor for mechanical repetition — worth knowing when flagging clichéd copy.

### Facts (§ 125)

Shared flaw of Russian designers and journalists: disregard for factual accuracy, source-checking, quote verification. **For both designer and journalist, any assignment becomes improvisation on a set of given keywords**, rather than fact-grounded work. **Someone who has something to say for themselves lets others hold their own opinion** — insecure people copy/misquote. Contrast: *Wired* employs dedicated fact-checkers, a standard largely absent in Russian publishing.

### Slogans (§ 30, § 160)

**Forcing a rhyme onto a brand name with no ear for language whatsoever** — reject slogans that exist only to rhyme, with no linguistic sensitivity behind them.

**Two widespread myths** (§ 160) responsible for more bad design/advertising than almost anything else: Myth 1 — **a good logo should be drawable from memory, by hand, by anyone** (false). Myth 2 — **it's good when an ad isn't instantly clear, because the decoding effort helps people remember it** (false). Clarity and genuine effectiveness beat forced simplicity or contrived obscurity.

### Robot-speak and automated text (§ 98)

Mass-produced automated text (mail-merge greetings, generic "read more" links, auto-truncated ellipses) reads as robotic — avoid or hand-finish it. Russian is harder to automate correctly than English due to its rich inflection; don't port English-style templated copy into Russian without editorial review. Named failure: the stilted, all-purpose greeting "good time of day" — a robotic dodge around choosing "good morning/afternoon/evening" — stands as the emblem of machine-like phrasing.

### Honest labeling (§ 127)

Serious publications must clearly label superstitious content (horoscopes) as such, rather than presenting it as credible reporting — the same way ads are marked "sponsored content." Calls astrology forecasts "unredeemable garbage" while granting entertainment value — insists on labels like "superstition" or "entertainment." (*Kommersant* actually discontinued horoscopes 12 January 2009.)

### Paper takes anything — printed carelessness (§ 128)

**Paper will tolerate it a second time, too** — paper's infinite tolerance gets exploited by publishers who show contempt for readers via careless printed content. Named failures: a translated design book that never converted imperial measurements to metric, forcing readers to calculate; a factory-director "interview" that reads like a raw spec sheet, suggesting no real dialogue occurred.

### E-mail culture — 10 commandments (§ 189)

1. Strip quoted headers ("On 18 Jan 2018, at 18:14, Vasya Pupkin wrote:") — **pure, one-hundred-percent garbage.**
2. Quote selectively — only the relevant portion, never the sender's signature block.
3. Separate your reply from quotes with blank lines; double-space between quoted sections.
4. **Your reply must be longer than what you quoted.**
5. Embed images directly in the message body — never send zipped image archives.
6. Keep messages under a couple of megabytes.
7. Don't bury text inside Word documents — the email body renders text fine.
8. Signatures: max 3–4 lines, no logos, no decorative icons.
9. Use your real full name or a clear identifiable nickname — not something generic like "Sasha."
10. Always fill the subject line, update it once the thread's topic shifts.

## 6. Working with clients and the profession

### The client is always wrong («Клиент всегда неправ») (§ 87)

- **The client is always wrong** — by default wrong on design questions, preoccupied with business concerns, not visual culture. The designer must hold a defensible position, not simply execute requests verbatim — closer to an "illusionist" delivering results that land with the real audience. **What matters is that the people the client actually deals with understand it — the people the design is really made for.**
- **Bad designers and bad clients** (§ 86): rejects the "client forced me" excuse — **bad design couldn't exist if bad designers didn't exist.** The designer bears final responsibility: block a bad concept or elevate a mediocre client idea into something that works.
- **"I want it like theirs"** (§ 103): clients copy a competitor's visual identity without grasping the strategic logic behind it. A mark like BMW's is good only because it sits on good cars — copying surface design without the underlying product excellence is like **eating the world marathon champion's kneecap to win a marathon yourself.** Design excellence is exemplary only in service of genuinely excellent products.
- **"What if the client is smarter than you?"** (§ 140): a designer is easily paralyzed by this question, which exposes that designer confidence rests on an unexamined assumption of superiority. Critiques the belief that a designer's work is Art deriving worth from authorship alone, rather than from solving real problems.

### Authorship is inalienable (§ 14, § 15)

- Authorship right arises at creation and is inalienable — it cannot be bought, sold, or gifted away, even when usage rights are sold. Selling a work transfers usage rights, not authorship: a designer keeps the right to show a commissioned logo in their portfolio even after the client owns exclusive usage. Plagiarism means copying idea, look, or solution with minimal change; working in someone's manner without copying specific elements violates nothing.
- The five marks — © ® ℗ ™ ℠ — are reminders only: **the presence of any of these symbols means absolutely nothing except as a reminder.** Their absence doesn't remove legal protection; misapplied/decorative use of © is **a mark of ignorance.** If proper glyphs are unavailable, (c)/(r)/(TM) text substitutes are poor form given modern typography — use the real character.

### Focus groups (§ 126)

- Marketing as **a multi-stage system for offloading accountability** — focus groups its chief instrument. Paid participants (≈500 rubles/hour) invent opinions to justify their presence; moderators suppress genuine expertise while amplifying buzzwords like "prestige"/"loyalty." **All you can learn by asking people's opinion is what they already knew before your product existed** — polling cannot predict genuine innovation. Segway and Apple bypassed focus groups entirely and still succeeded.
- **Related — research as dogma** (§ 130): eye-tracking studies showing users look at the upper-left aren't universal law — **people look wherever you show them something.** Attention follows content, not fixed zones. Reject the above-the-fold myth; scrolled-past content isn't forgotten. **Color is an ideological phenomenon** — color is contextual, not inherently symbolic; treating color meaning as fixed and universal is **shamanism worse than horoscopes.**
- **On marketing** (§ 115): entire thesis, unelaborated — **marketing does not exist** («Маркетинга не существует»).
- **The great marketing folly** (§ 96): don't let "research says" claims or folk-wisdom market opinions override professional judgment without real evidence. Case: Intel's global one-word "Yes" campaign, forced by Russian regulation to add a footnote translating it to "Yes" in Russian — the disclaimer semantically downgraded a confident statement into "yes, but maybe not." A mandated disclaimer can invert a design's core communicative intent — check what a required addition does to meaning, not just layout.

### Guidelines (§ 117)

- **Rule 1**: the guideline author must know exactly what to communicate and state it plainly — **the reader can't guess what knowledge stayed inside the author's head.** Spell out even the "obvious."
- **Rule 2**: guidelines need a competent interpreter who knows when rules can/should be broken and briefs the designer accordingly — not literal rule-following.
- **Writing guidelines thoughtlessly, and reading them literally, is a waste of time.** Bad guidelines pad themselves with meaningless symbolism ("round logo = perfection, square = stability"); good guidelines (BBC's pixel-grid explanation) teach the designer something they didn't already know.

### Tools instead of stock photos (§ 61)

**Using ready-made stock photography is poor form, if we're talking about real design work.** Under time/budget pressure, produce original material instead: scan or photograph real objects, hand-letter and scan text rather than using script fonts, stage real people/colleagues rather than generic stock models. Exception: stock is acceptable only for genuinely unobtainable imagery (space photography, archival footage) — and even then, prefer sourcing the primary material directly.

### Letterheads: the client is afraid of emptiness (§ 102)

A letterhead is a simple, utilitarian object needing no special decoration: **a letterhead is a very simple, utilitarian, functional object that requires no special decoration.** Clients mistake empty space for unfinished work — present letterheads filled with sample correspondence, not empty, to preempt the "it's empty" objection. Functional constraints override decoration: no background images (fax transmission), generous left margin (hole-punching), absorbent non-glossy paper (stamps/signatures). Prefer restraint — a subtle graphic echo over decorative excess.

### E-commerce doesn't save the offline business (§ 60)

**"Electronic commerce" doesn't exist** — rejects the term as marketing hype. A company must get its offline fundamentals right — delivery, service, logistics — before an online storefront can work; e-commerce doesn't fix broken commerce.

### Naming (§ 94)

- Four historical naming strategies, useful as a checklist: founder's name (Bosch, Chanel); geography (Nokia, Zhiguli); invented/unique letter combinations (Kodak, ABBYY); abbreviations (IKEA, SAAB). Successful naming is largely circumstantial, not formulaic — nobody ran focus groups for the brands that became iconic; be skeptical of naming decided by focus group or phonosemantic pseudo-science.
- Debunks the "25th frame" subliminal-message myth (Vicary's fabricated 1950s popcorn study; human perception can't resolve information at that speed) — a caution against basing design decisions on popular pseudoscience rather than professional judgment.

### The design brief (§ 95)

Reviewer's method: study failure, not success — **studying "successful" design gives you no way to understand how to avoid mistakes in the future.** Four-stage lifecycle, diagnostic lens: (1) a problem arises, (2) ordinary people notice/name it, (3) they improvise a DIY fix with available materials, (4) a professional solution finally replaces the improvisation. Scan the environment for visible DIY workarounds — each one is a live, undocumented design brief nobody has answered properly yet.

### Logos (§ 124, § 152, § 157)

- Word "logotype" drifted across three eras: 19th c. — synonym for a ligature (fused characters); late 19th c. — any reusable text plate (a masthead needing no resetting); 20th c. — anything: lettering, mark, or combination. Illustrated with Paul Rand's IBM mark, Rob Janoff's Apple logo, the Mercedes-Benz star.
- **Trademarks becoming everyday words** (§ 152): a healthy process, not a violation to resent. **It's good when a language is alive.** Insisting on generic terms (photocopier instead of Xerox, SUV instead of Jeep) is as absurd as calling cognac "brandy." ~80 former trademarks are now ordinary Russian words: adrenaline, aspirin, jeep, heroin, xerox, linoleum, thermos, escalator.
- **On stylization** (§ 157): one line, unqualified — **any stylization is terrible** («Любая стилизация — это ужасно»).

### Creative crisis, the comfort zone, and working conditions

- **Creative crisis** — see § 1 above (§ 162): crisis only arises from targeting originality as the goal; the original surfaces only while actually working the problem.
- **The comfort zone** (§ 172): differences in urban quality of life stem from psychology, not national wealth. **In Russia, the comfort zone traditionally ends at a person's own front door.** **Expanding it starts simply: let at least one more person into it.**
- **Pressure and freedom** (§ 179): managing creatives requires two independent controls, not one dial with opposite ends — **these are two different dials.** Too much of either pressure or freedom degrades output; balance is per-designer, per-project.
- **The aesthetics of control and prohibition** (§ 180): restrictive rules don't have to breed resentment. **Any control or prohibition can be presented elegantly enough that nobody feels humiliated by it.** **Something unpleasant or uninteresting should be packaged so that everyone wants it anyway.** Examples: Russian passports (blunt) vs. US driver's licenses as ID (normalized); bollards vs. gates; landscaping vs. fences.
- **An idea worth minus a million** (§ 161): ideas have no legal protection and inventors habitually overvalue them. Every real idea needs serious capital to become anything — **a minimum of a million** before any return. **Any inventor or designer talking about their idea should honestly say: "I have an idea worth minus a million."**
- **Softwarization** (§ 184): **everything that can be turned into software will be turned into software** (phone exchanges, water meters, books, posters, road markings, music's arc concerts→vinyl→streaming). Use this lens to judge which industries deserve investment and which are dissolving into software.
- **Everything gets worse over time** (§ 151): craftsmanship perceptibly declines across generations even as technology advances — anatomical atlas illustrations degrade from masterful (1900) to flat (1992) to incompetent (2007, artist doesn't know anatomy). Self-aware paradox: **it will be interesting to see how perception shifts once this very illustration is considered a model to imitate** — today's ugly outputs may look like tomorrow's gold standard.
- **Craft matters more than message** (§ 154): tracing the pointing-finger recruitment poster (Kitchener's "Your Country Needs YOU," Moor's "Have You Volunteered?"): **Moor wasn't just an outstanding artist — he was also a master of poster language.** Visual/rhetorical craft, not just the message, decides whether persuasive design actually works. A failed 1919 counter-version (formal "you," weak type) proves competence, not idea alone, decides effectiveness.
- **The pointless presentation** (§ 145): **the best presentation is the one that was never shown.** Slide format forces oversimplification; audiences read text faster than a speaker can say it aloud; Russian business culture is oral/dialogic and resists PowerPoint — real decisions happen after the slideshow, among leadership.

## Lebedev's razor — 10 sharpest one-line tests

1. **Has the task actually been solved?** — the only valid design question (§ 149): design isn't up for discussion, it *is* the solution to the stated task.
2. **Simple, but not primitive?** — did restraint keep the meaningful detail, or just remove consideration? (§ 147)
3. **Can it be cut in half?** — any interface can be simplified by half; find the redundant control/element before shipping. (§ 110)
4. **Would this survive detached from its medium?** — text off the browser, hypertext off its links, message off its channel — does it still make sense? (§ 83, § 141)
5. **Can a viewer spot it without touching the mouse?** — the link-visibility test, generalizable to any affordance. (§ 171)
6. **What's the worst possible version — and did you remember to flip it back?** — design by reversal; the flip, not just the imagining, is the design. (§ 150)
7. **Does this exist because it's cheaper than the alternative, or because it's actually needed?** — trash bins, bottlenecks, constraints: know the real economic/functional cause before redesigning. (§ 88, § 140)
8. **What does this do to the meaning, not just the layout?** — check what a mandated disclaimer, a client add-on, or a "safety" caveat does to the message's actual force. (§ 96)
9. **Would a security guard giving directions beat this signage system?** — the guard-can-just-tell-you test: if the wayfinding needs a manual, it has already failed. (§ 131)
10. **Is this a law, or just a guideline that worked once?** — in design there are no laws; interrogate every rule, including these, against the specific constraints in front of you. (§ 99)
