# Crisis — when reputation is under attack

No agent skill covering organizational crisis communications appeared to exist when this was written. What does exist under that name is the inverse: newsroom protocols for *covering* someone else's breaking news. So this file is written from the research rather than from the practice literature's folklore, and the two disagree more than you'd expect.

## Start here, before any tactic

**Attribution dominates response.** Ma & Zhan's meta-analysis found what you are blamed for correlates with reputation at r = −.54, while which response strategy you pick correlates at r = .23. Your rhetorical choices are real but second-order. The first question in any crisis meeting is therefore not "what do we say" but **"what is actually true, and what are we going to change?"** A team that spends its first hour on message architecture instead of facts has already made its worst decision.

**Apology has no special magic.** Coombs & Holladay (2008) tested apology, sympathy, and compensation head to head: statistically indistinguishable on reputation, anger, and negative word of mouth. All three clearly beat providing information alone. So the endless internal fight over whether to say "sorry" — usually driven by counsel's liability fear — is largely a false dilemma. **Respond accommodatively in some form; the specific form matters far less than doing it.**

**Steal thunder.** Self-disclosing before someone else discovers the problem produces better credibility, better reputation restoration, and more trust than being exposed. Replicated across several studies, though much of the evidence comes from legal contexts and crisis-specific replications are thinner. Coombs observes that an organization that steals thunder may not need a repair strategy at all. Practically: the instinct to wait, contain, and hope is the most expensive instinct in this discipline, and it is nearly universal.

## Diagnose the crisis type first

Coombs' Situational Crisis Communication Theory sorts crises by how much responsibility the public will attribute, which determines how accommodative your response must be. Three clusters, in ascending order of attributed blame:

| Cluster | Examples | Attributed responsibility | Response posture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Victim** | Natural disaster, rumor, workplace violence, product tampering | Minimal — you are also a victim | Inform and express concern; heavy accommodation is unnecessary and can imply guilt you don't have |
| **Accidental** | Technical-error accident or product harm, external challenges | Moderate | Explain the mechanism, correct it visibly, express regret |
| **Preventable** | Human-error accident or product harm, organizational misdeeds, management misconduct | High | Full accommodation: correction, compensation, apology. Anything less reads as evasion and deepens the damage |

Two amplifiers to check: **crisis history** (a repeat incident sharply intensifies attribution — a second occurrence is never treated as a first) and **prior reputation** (a genuine halo buys some protection, but it is spent, not renewed, by using it).

## The response strategies

Coombs' taxonomy, from least to most accommodative:

- **Deny** — attack the accuser, flat denial, scapegoating. Legitimate *only* when you are genuinely not responsible, most often in the Victim cluster. Used anywhere else it converts a survivable crisis into a credibility crisis, because the correction arrives later with your denial attached to it.
- **Diminish** — excuse (we didn't intend it, we couldn't have controlled it) or justification (the harm is smaller than claimed). Fits Accidental. Deploy carefully: minimizing harm to people who feel harmed is the fastest way to make the story about your response.
- **Rebuild** — compensation and apology. Required for Preventable.
- **Bolster** — reminding people of past good works, ingratiation, noting you are also a victim. **Supplemental only.** Used as a primary strategy it reads as changing the subject, because it is.

Benoit's Image Repair Theory offers a finer-grained vocabulary (denial, evading responsibility, reducing offensiveness, corrective action, mortification) and is useful for analyzing what someone *did* say. But it is rhetorical-critical rather than experimental, and Coombs has directly challenged its blanket prescription of mortification plus corrective action, which does not hold up across crisis types. **When they disagree, follow Coombs — he has the controlled studies.**

## The first hours

1. **Establish facts before establishing message.** Assign someone to own the timeline of what actually happened, separate from anyone writing copy. Most crisis mistakes are made by organizations that stated something confidently in hour one and had to retract it in hour six.
2. **Issue a holding statement fast, and keep it honest.** It should say what you know, what you don't yet know, what you're doing to find out, and when you'll update. It should not speculate about cause, assign blame, or minimize. The purpose is to occupy the space so the vacuum doesn't fill with worse.
3. **Prioritize by who is harmed, not by who is loudest.** Victims and affected people first, employees second — they will be asked about it and deserve not to learn from the news — then regulators and partners as required, then press. Getting this order wrong is itself a story.
4. **Pick one spokesperson.** Multiple voices produce contradictions, and contradictions become the second-day story.
5. **Fix the thing visibly.** See the meta-analysis. Corrective action is the part that moves the number.
6. **Update on your own schedule and keep it.** A promised update that doesn't arrive is read as concealment.

## Spokesperson discipline

Prepare the three or four questions you least want asked and answer them out loud before the interview, because the discomfort of hearing your own answer is the point. Never say "no comment" — it is universally received as an admission. If you cannot discuss something, say why you cannot, which is a real answer. Do not speculate about cause while an investigation is running; say that it's under investigation and what the investigation covers. Correct errors quickly and publicly rather than hoping they fade.

## Modern conditions the theory does not cover

SCCT was built for controlled, linear crises with identifiable publics and a media cycle. Its own critics note it does not account for viral escalation, decentralized online discourse, or algorithmic amplification of misinformation. Attribution is also culturally contingent, and much of the underlying research is US-sampled. Use it as the diagnostic spine; do not expect it to predict how a pile-on behaves.

## Learn from the disputed case, not the myth

Tylenol 1982 is taught as the profession's gold standard: rapid nationwide recall, credo-driven decisiveness. Senior practitioners have publicly disputed the details — the strategy committee reportedly convened days after the deaths, not hours; the initial recall was Chicago-area, going national only after a second poisoning; the underlying vulnerability was repackaged rather than eliminated; and the seven people who died are almost never named in a retelling that centers corporate decision-making. The field's institutional body dismissed this critique, so it remains contested.

Either way, the lesson for you is the same and it is not about speed of messaging. **The case is remembered because the company withdrew a profitable product rather than defend it.** That is a conduct decision, not a communications decision, which is exactly what the evidence says drives reputation.
